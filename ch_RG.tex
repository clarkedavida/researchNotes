\chapter{Physics: Phase transitions}

In \chref{ch:statphys} we saw that in a statistical mechanical system,
observables of interest can be derived from the free energy or grand potential,
i.e. from $\log\mathcal{Z}$. During this discussion there was more or less an
assumption that $\log\mathcal{Z}$ was mathematically well behaved, or at least
I never brought it up. In general $\log\mathcal{Z}$ may not be smooth
everywhere. A point in the space of control parameters where $\log\mathcal{Z}$
is not well behaved is a {\it critical point}\index{critical!point}. Depending
on the system under study, this may even be extended to a {\it critical line} or
{\it critical surface}; generically we refer to these as {\it phase
boundaries}\index{phase boundary}. These shapes separate the space of control parameters
into different regions where $\log\mathcal{Z}$ is analytic. Each of these
regions corresponds to a {\it phase}\index{phase} or {\it state}\index{state} of
the system. Physically, the system exhibits qualitative differences between
different phases. When the system crosses this critical boundary and moves from
one phase to another, it is said to undergo a {\it phase transition}\index{phase
transition}.
Much of our current understanding of phase transitions was developed by studying
liquid-gas systems and ferromagnets. 

Critical temperatures in fluids were discovered by T. 
Andrews\footnote{He seems to be the one who coined the
term ``critical point".}~\cite{andrews_bakerian_1869}.
J. D. van der Waals\footnote{Apparently, van der Waals grew up working
class, which at that time in Amsterdam precluded him from attending
university. As a result, he started off as a school teacher and did not
obtain his PhD until he was in his late 30s. His PhD thesis seems to be
the work that won him the 1910 Nobel prize.}
was, to my knowledge, the first to attempt to
model the existence of criticality~\cite{van_der_waals_over_1873}.
Beginning with such work, analyses of critical singularities 
focused largely on static properties, such as equilibrium
expectation values. However it is also the case that dynamical properties,
such as transport coefficients and responses to time-dependent perturbations,
which cannot be derived from the equilibrium distribution, also contain
critical behavior. In this context one can distinguish between
{\it static critical phenomena} and {\it dynamic critical phenomena}.

These research notes were made from the perspective of a researcher working
in lattice field theory, and one advantage of the lattice, which will be
discussed later in \chref{ch:preliminaries}, is that it allows one
to make direct analogies with statistical mechanical systems, and in
particular, to utilize powerful techniques like renormalization group (RG)
analysis. On the lattice, one is dealing with the equilibrium distribution,
and hence we will restrict our attention to only static critical 
phenomena\footnote{If you want to learn about dynamic critical phenomena, a very
friendly introduction can be found in Ref.~\cite{halperin_theory_2019}.
A landmark paper introduces the classification system for dynamical
critical phenomena is Ref.~\cite{hohenberg_theory_1977}.}.

This chapter will generally try to follow 
lectures by F. Karsch, as well as the book by Binney et
al.~\cite{binney_theory_1992}. 

\section{Types of transitions}\label{sec:typesOfTransitions}

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/simplified_water.png}
\caption{Simplified phase diagram for water indicating its solid, liquid, and
gas phases. Phase boundaries are indicated by the black lines. It is difficult
to see on the scale of this plot, but the solid-liquid boundary near 1 atm has a
slight lean to the left, which is an uncommon feature for most substances having
a solid-liquid transition. Beyond the
critical point, liquid and gas phases cannot be distinguished. 
Image taken from Wikipedia \cite{Wiki_water}.}
\label{fig:phase_water}
\end{figure}

The definition of phase transition given in the introduction is mathematically
unambiguous, but it may not help give much physics intuition. To this end, it is
useful to think about some archetypical substances that exhibit many of the
properties common to systems undergoing phase transitions.

In \figref{fig:phase_water} we see a simplified {\it phase diagram}\index{phase
diagram} for water. A phase diagram shows the various phases where
the free energy is analytic, separated by phase boundaries as discussed before.
Phase transitions can be classified according to how the free energy and its
derivatives behave when crossing a phase boundary. In the case of water, these
are {\it first-order}\footnote{This naming scheme dates back to Paul Ehrenfest's
classification of phase transitions. Under his scheme, a transition is said to
be of order $n$ if the $n\nth$ derivative of the free energy is discontinuous
across the phase boundary.} phase transitions\index{phase transition!first-order}.
In general when changing a control parameter, one has to dump energy into the
system; for instance increasing temperature generically requires the addition of
heat. When the system hits a first-order phase boundary, energy dumped into the
system will, for a brief period, not change the control parameter, and instead
is needed to change the phase of the substance. In the case of water, when
hitting for instance the solid-liquid first-order line, additional heat is used
to break the bonds between molecules, changing the substance from a solid into a
liquid. The energy required to do this is the {\it latent heat}\index{latent
heat}. The existence of a latent heat defines first-order phase transitions.

Along phase boundaries, different phases may coexist\footnote{This
coexistence, which in a sense occurs because there is a latent heat,
can also be used to characterize first-order transitions.}. For instance along the
solid-liquid phase boundary one will in general find a mixture of solids and
liquid, which is sometimes called a {\it slurry}\index{slurry}. 
Shown in \figref{fig:phase_water} is also the {\it triple point}\index{triple
point}, where all three of the solid, liquid, and gas phases coexist.
We use common verbiage when crossing the boundaries: the substance {\it
melts}\index{melt} when going from solid to liquid, {\it
evaporates}\index{evaporate} when going
from liquid to gas, and {\it sublimates}\index{sublimate} when going from solid
to gas. Salient in \figref{fig:phase_water} is also a critical point, beyond
which liquid and gas cannot be distinguished. One sometimes refers to this
region as a {\it supercritical fluid}\index{supercritical fluid}. A path from
liquid to gas region going around the critical point, not crossing any phase
boundary, allows for a analytic change from liquid to gas. Such a change of
phase is referred to as a {\it crossover}\index{crossover}. Since this change is
completely analytic, we will distinguish crossovers from phase transitions in
these notes.

\caption{Phase diagram for the \dimens{2} Ising model. The solid black
circle indicates the critical temperature; the solid line connected to it is a
first-order line. Magnetization is indicated by $M$.
Image taken from Ref.~\cite{ising_picture}.}
\label{fig:phase_ising}
\end{figure}

The liquid-gas line for water 
gives the quintessential example of a first-order phase transition. Now we
will consider {\it second-order} or {\it continuous} phase
transitions\index{phase transition!second-order}\index{phase
transition!continuous}. The Ising model of a ferromagnet in two or more
dimensions is the quintessential example for second-order transitions.
The \dimens{2} {\it Ising model}\index{Ising model} 
imagines a magnet as being comprised of interacting spins
with nearest-neighbor interactions. Its Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
H=-J\sum_{\ev{i j}} \sigma_i \sigma_j-h \sum_j
\sigma_j,
\end{equation}
where the brackets indicate a sum over nearest-neighbors,
$\sigma_i\in\{+1,-1\}$ indicates the value of spin $i$, $h$ gives the
coupling of the spins to an external magnetic field, and $J$ gives the
nearest-neighbor interaction strength.

The Ising model phase diagram in two dimensions is given 
in \figref{fig:phase_ising}.
At fixed temperature, when varying the external magnetic field, one can
discontinuously flip the magnetization $M$. Moving from left to right in $T$
at $h=0$, this first-order line terminates
in a second-order critical point. When
approaching the critical point by varying the temperature, one can
show\footnote{This was first done by Onsager~\cite{onsager_crystal_1944}. A
nice, closed-form solution like this is not available for three dimensions or
more. Critical exponents are typically extracted from Monte Carlo simulations.}
that the magnetization changes as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exampleCP}
  M\sim|T-T_c|^\beta
\end{equation}
with $\beta=1/8$. This sort of behavior is a hallmark of second-order
transitions. The quantity $\beta$ is an example of a {\it critical
exponent}\index{critical exponent}. 
Many other physical quantities will also exhibit asymptotic behavior when
approaching a second-order point analogous to \equatref{eq:exampleCP}.
These will have their own critical exponents. These
critical exponents can be related to each other, which we discuss
in \secref{sec:scalingHypothesis}.

What is positively remarkable is that completely different
physical systems sometimes have exactly the same critical exponents.
This phenomenon is known as {\it universality}\index{universal}, and when two
systems have the same exponents, they are said to belong to the same {\it
universality class}\index{universality class}. In the context of lattice QCD,
it has for example been demonstrated that the chiral transition for $N_f=2+1$
quark masses in the chiral limit $m_u=m_d\equiv m_l\to0$ belongs to the
\dimens{3}, $\ON(4)$ universality class~\cite{ding_chiral_2019}, 
the same class as a generalized magnet with spins
free to rotate on \dimens{4} hyperspheres.

For the Ising model, at extremely high temperatures, 
the $TS$ term in the free energy dominates over
the internal energy, and the spins of the ferromagnet become, in the absence of
an external magnetic field, randomly aligned\footnote{In the high-temperature
phase, one can point the spins in a particular direction by applying an external
field.}, and hence the net magnetization is zero. If one decreases the
temperature below $T_c$, the internal energy coming from spin alignment becomes
more important, and domains of aligned spins start to emerge. Eventually the
system will settle pointing in one direction. There is nothing special about the
direction the system picks; this is an example of spontaneous symmetry
breaking\index{spontaneous symmetry breaking}, which plays a major role in QFT,
as discussed in \secref{sec:ssb}. 


First-order lines terminate in second-order 
endpoints\footnote{It seems to be that
all critical points are attached to first-order (coexistence) lines
(or surfaces depending on how many control parameters you consider).
This can be used to intuitively guess the universality class of
the critical point. For example critical points touching only two phases
regardless of the number of extra parameters you introduce would
belong to $\Z_2$. The situation seems to be more subtle for three phases.
If it is true that all critical points are attached to coexistence
lines/surfaces, one can argue that the sometimes-used jargon
``critical endpoint" is redundant.}.
Indeed, this
can be seen in both \figref{fig:phase_water} and \figref{fig:phase_ising}.
At this critical endpoint, the coexistence line fuses into one phase.
Another possibility is a {\it tricritical point}\index{tricritical point}
where three phases fuse. This can be seen in water's phase diagram, but
does not exist in the \dimens{2} Ising model diagram.
Generally for liquid-gas transitions like
water, the expectation is that the endpoint belongs to the Ising
model universality
class~\cite{hubbard_wilson_1972,bruce_scaling_1992,wilding_critical-point_1995,yarmolinsky_revisiting_2017}.
Still, this statement is not without some controversy, with some studies suggesting
it has a universality class of its own, albeit with very similar
critical exponents~\cite{valls_liquid-gas_1978}.

\subsection{Percolation}\label{sec:percolation}

Here I formulate percolation following a nice paper that describes it
very readably~\cite{Fortunato:1999xj}. Imagine placing black stones randomly on
a Go board of area $V$. Contiguous regions of stones are called {\it
clusters}\index{cluster}. If a cluster spans the Go board from one side to the
opposite, this cluster {\it percolates}.\index{percolation}
Clusters can be characterized by their average cluster size $S$
\begin{equation}
S\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_s n_s s^2,~~~~~~ N\equiv \sum_s n_s s,
\end{equation} 
where the sum runs over cluster size $s$, $n_s$ is the number of clusters of
this size, and the sum excludes percolating clusters. Suppose a cluster
of size $s_p$ percolates. Then the {\it percolation
strength}\index{strength!percolation} $P$ is
\begin{equation}
P\equiv \frac{s_p}{V}.
\end{equation}
Changing the density of the stones induces a kind of phase transition. To see this,
note that if there are too few stones, there can be no percolation by
definition. Eventually, a cluster must percolate, and $P$ changes from 0 to some
nonzero value, and $P$ can be viewed as an order parameter of a
{\it percolation phase transition}\index{phase transition!percolation}.



\section{Models of magnets}\label{sec:magnets}

In 2009, the philosophers Violent J. and Shaggy 2 Dope posed the quintessential
question: {\it Fucking magnets--how do they work?} In this section we will attempt 
an answer by examining a handful of classical magnet models. We first remind 
the reader of a few basic facts about electromagnetism.

Classically, the torque $\vec\tau$ on a magnetic dipole due to an external
magnetic field $\vec B$ is characterized by its {\it magnetic moment}\index{magnetic moment} $\vec m$:
\begin{equation}
\vec\tau = \vec m\times\vec B.
\end{equation}
Using that the is the negative gradient of a potential energy, we find
\begin{equation}\label{eq:magneticmomentU}
U=-\vec m\cdot\vec B.
\end{equation}
The energy is minimized when the magnetic moment aligns with the field.
For uniform $\vec B$, the torque is minus the derivative of $U$ w.r.t. the angle
between $\vec m$ and $\vec B$, i.e. the torque tends to align the dipole with
the field.

There seems to be no magnetic monopoles in nature, so a common, classical,
microscopic model imagines a small current loop. From this perspective, the
magnetic moment is 
\begin{equation}
\vec m=I\vec{A},
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the current and $A$ the area enclosed by the loop.
This can be generalized such that a distribution of electric charge undergoing
orbital motion with angular momentum $\vec L$ behaves like a current loop with
magnetic moment
\begin{equation}\label{eq:magneticmomentL}
\vec m=\gamma\vec L,
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ is a constant.
Thinking about quantum physics, \equatref{eq:magneticmomentL} implies that a
particle carrying spin, which can be thought of as an intrinsic angular
momentum, carries an intrinsic magnetic moment with it. 
In particular the intrinsic magnetic moment coming from a particle's spin turns
out to be
\begin{equation}\label{eq:magneticmomentS}
\vec m = g\frac{q}{2m}\vec\sigma,
\end{equation}
where $q$ is the charge, $m$ is the mass, and $\vec \sigma$ is the spin, and $g$
is the {\it gyromagnetic ratio}\index{gyromagnetic ratio}.

The preceding discussion motivates the following kind of magnet model: One
imagines a material as a collection of particles with spin that are 
arranged on the sites of an 
$n$-dimensional lattice. The simplest models imagine hypercubic lattices.
The macroscopic {\it magnetization}\index{magnetization} is the sum over all 
the magnetic moments of the particles. Assuming they are all the same particle
and ignoring the constant factors, this amounts to summing over the spins, so that 
the magnetization becomes
\begin{equation}
\vec{M}=\sum_i\vec\sigma_i,
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over sites. Up to now we have included explicit vector
symbols, but we will drop them going forward, which is conventional. 

In \secref{sec:typesOfTransitions} we introduced the Ising\footnote{This
model is named after Ernst Ising, who was given the problem by his
PhD advisor Wilhelm Lenz, who conceived it. Ising was Jewish and
fled from Germany to Luxembourg during the pogroms. Luxembourg was
occupied by the Nazis, and during that time he was a shepherd and did
railroad work. After the allied liberation, he emigrated to the US,
where he became a physics professor in Peoria, IL.} model 
Hamiltonian\footnote{One can generalize this model replacing
$J\to J_{ij}$ and $h\to h_j$. However the most well studied and easiest
to implement models take these interactions to be uniform throughout
the lattice, as presented in \equatref{eq:ising}. Such generalizations
are of course also possible for the subsequent models we present.}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ising}
H=-J\sum_{\ev{i j}} \sigma_i \sigma_j-h \sum_j
\sigma_j,
\end{equation}
where the brackets indicate a sum over nearest-neighbors,
$\sigma_i\in\{+1,-1\}$ is the spin $i$, $h$ is 
external magnetic field, and $J$ is the
nearest-neighbor interaction strength. 
This model tries to capture \equatref{eq:magneticmomentU}, i.e. the
idea that spins in a magnet prefer to align, as $H$ lowers when
$\sigma_i$ and $\sigma_j$ have the same sign. Similarly when there
is an external field, the model prefers spins to align with it.
We note that, generally speaking, the critical behavior of a magnet model
depends on the geometry of its supporting space. In this section, we assume the
spins lie on square, cubic, or hypercubic lattices.

We said this was the \dimens{2} 
Ising model, but since we are labelling the sites by indices, the form
will be the same for any $d\in\N$. The \dimens{1} model was shown to
exhibit no phase transition by Ising himself~\cite{ising_beitrag_1924}.
The \dimens{2} model was solved analytically by 
Onsager~\cite{onsager_crystal_1944}, who found a critical temperature of
\begin{equation}
\Tc=\frac{2 J}{k_B \log (1+\sqrt{2})}\approx(2.269185) \frac{J}{k_B}.
\end{equation}
Generally particle physicists work in units where $k_B=1$. Moreover
to better expose quantities like $\Tc$, we often set $J=1$. Under this
convention, one then gets $\Tc$ in the form most commonly quoted
in the literature,
\begin{equation}
\Tc\approx2.269185.
\end{equation}

There is no analytic solution to the Ising model in $n$ dimensions, when $n>2$.
Typically we rely on the results of Markov chain Monte Carlo
simulations\footnote{We discuss such simulations in detail applied to lattice
field theory in \chref{ch:MCMC}.}. Some $\Tc$ for the \dimens{3} 
Ising model are collected in the first column of
\tabref{tab:pottsTc}. 

Generic spin models are essentially just generalizations of \equatref{eq:ising}.
The next standard generalization is called a $q$-state
{\it Potts model}\index{Potts model}\footnote{The 4-state Potts model
is sometimes referred to as the {\it Ashkin-Teller model}\index{Ashkin-Teller
model}, as they had studied an equivalent model already
in 1943~\cite{ashkin_statistics_1943}.} or $S_q$ {\it model}, originally studied by 
Potts~\cite{Potts:1951rk}. The Hamiltonian\footnote{One can also add 
an external magnetic field term.} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:potts}
H=-J\sum_{\ev{i j}} \delta\left(\sigma_i, \sigma_j\right)
\end{equation}
where now $\sigma_i\in\{0,1,...,q-1\}$. In this
model, the system favors exact spin alignment, with all other configurations
equally disfavored. Now both $q$ and $d$ are adjustable parameters of the
theory. We see that the 2-state Potts model and the Ising model with
zero magnetic field are essentially equivalent. In fact, sometimes a
{\it normalization}\index{normalization} or offset is added to $H$
so that one finds $\ev{E}=0$ in the completely disordered
(random spin orientation) phase, in agreement
with what happens for the Ising model.
Estimates of the critical temperature for some \dimens{3} Potts
models are collected in \tabref{tab:pottsTc}.
In \dimens{2}, the model is exactly 
solvable~\cite{baxter_exactly_1982}. It is a first-order
transition when $q>4$ and second-order when $q\leq4$.
The critical temperature in units $J=k_B=1$ is given by
\begin{equation}
\Tc=\frac{1}{\log\left(1+\sqrt{q}\right)},
\end{equation}
which we see is closely related to the Ising $T_c$ when $q=2$, as expected.

Reflecting a bit on electrodynamics, perhaps a more physically accurate theory
would allow a continuous penalty for spin unalignment. This leads to
the $q$-state {\it clock model}\index{clock model}, 
{\it vector Potts model}\index{Potts model!vector},
or $\Z_q$ {\it model}, with Hamiltonian 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:clock}
H=-J\sum_{\ev{i j}} \cos\left(\theta_i-\theta_j\right)
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\theta_i\equiv\frac{2\pi\sigma_i}{q}
\end{equation}
and $\sigma_i\in\{0,1,...,q-1\}$ as before.
In the limit $q\to\infty$, one obtains the so-called
{\it XY model}\index{XY model} or $\ON(2)$ model.
It is interesting to note that for $d\leq2$, the
$\ON(2)$ has no phase transition. This is a special case of a more general
result known as the {\it Mermin-Wagner theorem}\index{Mermin-Wagner theorem},
which states that there can be no stable ordered phase at finite
temperature in $d\leq2$ for any system with a continuous 
symmetry~\cite{mermin_absence_1966}.
Interestingly enough, while there is no phase transition in the usual sense,
there is still some kind of phase change.
In particular spins can arrange themselves in vortices and antivortices.
Kosterlitz, Thouless, and independently Berezinskii were able to show that there
is a low-temperature phase where vortices and antivortices must arrange 
themselves in pairs along with a high-temperature phase where vortices can
exist in 
isolation~\cite{Berezinsky:1972rfj,kosterlitz_ordering_1973,kosterlitz_critical_1974}.
For example in the $\ON(2)$ model, a {\it vortex}\index{vortex} can be found
if one follows the spins around an elementaray plaquette, summing their relative
angles along the way, and get $2\pi$. An {\it antivortex} state sums to $-2\pi$.
Vortex and antivortex states are shown
schematically in \figref{fig:classicalExcitations} (b).

The $\ON(2)$ model imagines each spin as lying on a unit circle. These spins can be
even further generalized. In particular when we allow each spin to lie on a unit
sphere, we obtain the {\it Heisenberg model}\index{Heisenberg model}
or $\ON(3)$ model. With MCMC one can find in three dimensions
$\Tc=1.44321(21)$~\cite{peczak_high-accuracy_1991}
and 1.44300(21)~\cite{holm_critical_1993}.
General $N$-spin systems go by the name of $\ON(N)$ models.

The Blume-Capel\index{Blume-Capel model} 
model~\cite{blume_theory_1966,capel_possibility_1966} is given
by the Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}\label{eq:blume-capel}
H=-J\sum_{\ev{i j}} \sigma_i \sigma_j+D \sum_j\sigma_j^2,
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_i\in\{-1,0,1\}$. For this model, the coupling $D$ is called
the {\it crystal-field coupling}, which is attached to a term quadratic in the
fields. This model is sometimes interesting because it features a second-order
line that becomes first-order for low $T$.

Up to now we have considered magnet models featuring only a nearest-neighbor
interaction, however it may be useful for such systems to consider
next-to-nearest-neighbor interactions. Such interactions can be useful to study,
e.g., geometric {\it frustration}\index{frustration}, a general phenomenon in
which competing inter-atomic forces lead to intricate structures such as
highly degenerate ground states. In the \dimens{2} {\it frustrated Ising model}\index{Ising
model!frustrated}, one has the Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
H=J_1\sum_{\ev{i j}} \sigma_i \sigma_j + J_2\sum_{\ev{\ev{i j}}} \sigma_i \sigma_j,
\end{equation}
where $\ev{\ev{ij}}$ indicates second, diagonal neighbors on the square lattice
and $J_1$ and $J_2$ are chosen to have opposite signs. In this context, one
usually defines a parameter $g\equiv J_2/|J_1|$ to capture the relative strength
of the couplings. For $g<1/2$ at $T=0$, the system exhibits a standard
ferromagnetic ground state. For $g>1/2$, the system energetically punishes
alignment with the second, diagonal neighbors, and the spins organize themselves
into aligned and anti-aligned stripes, an example of a highly degenerate ground
state emerging in a frustrated system. This structure is called the
{\it stripe anti-ferromagnetic phase}\index{stripe anti-ferromagnetic}.
The phase structure for $g>1/2$ is somewhat controversial: It is
unclear whether it is second-order $\forall g>1/2$ or whether there exists a
first-order region. Moreover the universality classes to which these systems
belong is still under active study.


\section{Singularity structure}\label{sec:leeyang}\index{Lee-Yang!Theorem}

One of the lessons of \chref{ch:statphys} is that the partition function of a
system determines all its observables. For example in the grand canonical
formulation, we saw that the grand potential is related to the partition
function by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grandPotZ}
  \Phi\left(V,T,\vec{\mu}\right)=-T\log\grandZ.
\end{equation}
Remaining observables, such as energy densities, entropy, heat capacity, and so
on, can then in principle be determined by carefully taking partial derivatives
and utilizing relations like the first law of thermodynamics or the Gibbs-Duhem
relation.

\subsection{The Lee-Yang theorem}

The partition function is also useful to find
phase transitions. Perhaps the most important work in this regard is by Lee and
Yang~\cite{yang_statistical_1952}, who demonstrated that, in the 
{\it thermodynamic limit}\index{limit!thermodynamic} limit, i.e.
$V\to\infty$, $\grandZ(h)$, which we write as a function of the magnetization or
symmetry-breaking parameter $h$, will have zeros at phase transitions. According
to \equatref{eq:grandPotZ} then, we correspondingly expect 
singularities in the free energy\footnote{When it comes to the Ising model,
it is somewhat intuitive that the thermodynamic limit is a necessary
condition for phase transitions. At finite volume, the partition function
is a finite sum of spins scaled by $T$, and hence must be an analytic
function of $T$. This guarantee is lost when one considers infinite sums.}. 
In their original paper, they allow the fugacity, which they denote
as $y$, to be complex, then imagine the situation in the complex plane.
The regions of analyticity for $y$ along with the expected behavior
of the pressure and its $\mu$-derivatives is shown schematically
in \figref{fig:leeYangTheorem}, which is taken from Lee and Yang. 


Let's discuss the Lee-Yang theorem in a bit more detail. Following
Ref.~\cite{blythe_lee-yang_2003}, we will use a simple
lattice model of $N$ spins in contact with a thermal reservoir. There, they
consider complex temperature instead of external field, but the core of the
argument is the same. Since
there are $N$ spins this is a system at finite volume by definition. The allowed
energies can be written $E=n\epsilon$ where $0\leq n\leq M$ and 
$n,M\in\Z$. Let the number of
microstates corresponding to level $n$ be $g(n)$. Then the partition function is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ZNsimple}
\grandZ_N=\sum_{n=0}^M g(n)e^{-\beta n\epsilon}.
\end{equation}
Introduce $z\equiv e^{-\beta\epsilon}$. For $\beta\in\R$, $z>0$.
Then by the fundamental theorem of
algebra, we can rewrite $\grandZ_N$
\begin{equation}
\grandZ_N=\sum_{n=1}^M g(n)z^n = \kappa\prod_{n=1}^M\left(1-\frac{z}{z_n}\right),
\end{equation}
where $z_n\in\C$ are the zeros of $\grandZ_N$ and $\kappa$ is an irrelevant
overall factor. By \equatref{eq:ZNsimple}, the fact
that $g(n)>0$ $\Forall n$ ensures $\grandZ_N$ is strictly positive, and thus none of
the roots can lie on the real axis. 

We next write the free energy
\begin{equation}
f=\frac{\log(\grandZ_N)}{N}.
\end{equation}
Plugging in our polynomial for $\grandZ_N$, dropping the irrelevant constant factor
that only serves to shift the zero point of the free energy, we get
\begin{equation}
f_N=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^M\log\left(1-\frac{z}{z_n}\right).
\end{equation}
Note that away from the zeroes, this free energy is analytic, and thus
arbitrarily many times differentiable. We identify phase transitions through
discontinuities in derivatives of $f_N$ at positive,
real values of the control parameter,
in this case $\beta$. Since none of the $z_n$ is real, there is no such
discontinuity on the real axis. We could still spoil the differentiability 
at a point $z_0$ on the real axis if, for any neighborhood of $z_0$, we
can find a $z_n$ in that neighborhood, but that there are finitely many $z_n$
forbids this scenario, too.
We therefore learn the following important statement:

\begin{theorem}{Lee-Yang}{}
There are no phase transitions at finite volume.
\end{theorem}

This theorem is completely generic to any lattice system whose partition
function can be cast in the form \eqref{eq:ZNsimple}. If we want want a phase
transition at $z_0$, we would need to zeros to become dense enough near
$z_0$ that we can always find them in neighborhoods of the point. 
Another useful fact one can see from \equatref{eq:ZNsimple} is the following:

\begin{theorem}{}{}
If $z_0$ is a zero of $\grandZ$ then so is $z_0^*$.
\begin{proof}
Let $\grandZ(z_0)=0$. Then since the $g(n)$ are real,
$$
\grandZ(z_0^*)
=\sum_ng(n)\left(z_0^*\right)^n
=\sum_ng(n)\left(z_0^n\right)^*
=\left(\sum_ng(n)z_0^n\right)^*
=0.
$$
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\subsection{First- and second-order transitions}

Let's see what happens
in the infinite-volume limit, where $N\to\infty$. We assume the limit
\begin{equation}
f=\lim_{N\to\infty} f_N
\end{equation} 
exists, that this limit drives $M\to\infty$, and that the density
\begin{equation}
\rho_N(z')\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^M\delta(z'-z_n)
\end{equation}
approaches a smooth function $\rho$, the {\it density of zeroes},\index{density
of zeroes} in this limit.\footnote{These statements
can be shown rigorously~\cite{ruelle1999statistical}.}
Then the free energy can be written as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:densityZeroIntegral}
f=\int\dd{z'}\rho(z')\log\left(1-\frac{z}{z'}\right).
\end{equation}
Here, writing $z=x+iy$, we have used that
\begin{equation}
\delta(z'-z_n)=\delta(x'-x_n)\delta(y'-y_n);
\end{equation}
thus we are interpreting \equatref{eq:densityZeroIntegral} as a 2D area integral
over the complex plane, i.e. $\dd{z}=\dd{x}\dd{y}$ is real. Therefore the 
potential, which we define as the real part of the free energy, comes out to 
be\footnote{The easiest way to show this is to write $z=Re^{i\theta}$ and go
from there.}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LYpotential}
\phi(z)\equiv\Re f(z)
=\int\dd{z'}\rho(z')\Re\log\left(1-\frac{z}{z'}\right)
=\int\dd{z'}\rho(z')\log\left|1-\frac{z}{z'}\right|.
\end{equation}

At this stage, it is worthwhile to point out a subtle but important
difference compared to the finite-volume case: Since we assumed $\rho$
approached a smooth function, this logarithmic potential is continuous
everywhere, also at the zeros of $\grandZ$~\cite{KelloggPot}. In the integral,
the singularities at $z_n$ now have measure zero, such that the potential
remains well behaved.\footnote{Basically the same reason that
$\int_0^1\dd{x}\log{x}$ is integrable.}  
We will need to exploit the continuity of $\phi$ later, and as always, our
phase transitions will be defined by discontinuities in derivatives of
the free energy.

We are now going to work toward an understanding of how the partition function
zeros are arranged in the complex plane in the thermodynamic limit. To
accomplish this, we will use \equatref{eq:LYpotential} to write $\rho$ as a
function of $\phi$, then use our knowledge of $\phi$ to learn about $\rho$.
We will see that $\rho$ and $\phi$ are formally related the same way as a charge
density and potential in electrostatics, hence the nomenclature for $\phi$.

First note that $\log|z|$ is the Green function for Laplace's equation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:greenLaplace}
\nabla^2\log|z|=2\pi\delta(z).
\end{equation}
To see this, note that $\nabla^2\log|z|=0$ away from $z=0$. To reveal its
identity as a delta function, you can integrate in a neighborhood $D$
of radius $r$ about $z=0$:
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
\int_D\dd{z}\nabla^2\log|z|
&=\frac{1}{2}\int_D\dd{x}\dd{y}\nabla\cdot\left(\nabla\log\left(x^2+y^2\right)\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\oint_{\partial D}\dd{\vec n}\cdot\nabla\log\left(x^2+y^2\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int_0^{2\pi}\hat{r}r\dd{\theta}\cdot\hat{r}\pdv{}{r}\log{r}\\
&=\int_0^{2\pi}\dd{\theta} =2\pi.
\end{aligned}\end{equation}
Here we used the divergence theorem, so $\dd{\vec n}$ points normal to the
contour, and switched to cylindrical coordinates. Plugging
\equatref{eq:greenLaplace} into \equatref{eq:LYpotential} and reminding
ourselves that the Laplacian acts on $z$ rather than $z'$ yields 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LYpoisson}
\rho(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\nabla^2\phi(z),
\end{equation} 
which gives us Poisson's equation, i.e. our electrostatics analogy.


\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.4\linewidth]{figs/LYpotential.pdf}
\caption{The potential $\phi$ with domain in the complex plane.
A small area $A$ covers an infinitesimal segment $\delta s$
of the curve $C$ of zeros. $s=0$ corresponds to $z_0$.
Image taken from Ref.~\cite{blythe_lee-yang_2003}.}
\label{fig:LYpotential}
\end{figure}

Just like in electrostatics, if we integrate \equatref{eq:LYpotential} over any
bounded region containing singularities of $\rho$, $\phi$ is guaranteed to be a
continuous function~\cite{KelloggPot}. If there is a critical point $z_0$
on the real axis, then we expect two different phases, one above and one below
$\Re z_0$. In general we then have two different free energies, corresponding to
two different potentials. Let $\phi_1$ label the potential in the region ``to
the left" of $z_0$ and $\phi_2$ label the other phase; see
\figref{fig:LYpotential}. That $\phi$ is continuous then requires
$\phi_1(z)=\phi_2(z)$ along the phase boundary, i.e. along a curve $C$
going through $z_0$, reaching into the complex plane following the density of
zeros $\rho$. Since phase transitions manifest as discontinuities in derivatives
of the free energy, or equivalently since $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$ are different
functions, we generally expect some derivative of $\phi$ to not exist at points
along $z$ where the zeros are.

Now parameterize $C$ with $s$ and consider an infinitesimal segment 
$\delta s$ of this curve, enclosed by an
area $A$ with two sides parallel to $C$ and two sides perpendicular, as
in \figref{fig:LYpotential}. The line density of zeros $\mu$, i.e. the density
of zeros along $C$, at $s$ is
\begin{equation}
\mu(s)\delta s=\int_A\dd z\rho(z).
\end{equation}
Using \equatref{eq:LYpoisson} and the divergence theorem yields
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}\label{eq:LYarea}
\int_A\dd{z}\rho(z)
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_A\dd{z}\nabla\cdot\nabla\phi(z)\\
&=\frac{\delta s}{2\pi}\left(\nabla\phi_2(z^+)-\nabla\phi_1(z^-)\right)\cdot\hat{n},
\end{aligned}\end{equation}
where $\hat n$ is the unit vector normal to $C$ at $z$ and $z^{\pm}$ denotes
the limit approaching $z$ from the $\pm\hat{n}$ side.\footnote{$+\hat{n}$
lies on the $\phi_2$ side.} 
Let $\psi$ be the imaginary part of the free energy $f$. 
In the context of \equatref{eq:LYarea}, the CR equations apply, so we can write
\begin{equation}
\partial_x\phi=\partial_y\psi~~~~\text{and}~~~~\partial_y\phi=-\partial_x\psi,
\end{equation}
from which it follows that
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
\hat{n}\cdot\nabla\phi(z)
&=\hat{n}\cdot\left(\hat{x}\partial_x+\hat{y}\partial_y\right)\phi(z)\\
&=\hat{n}\cdot\left(\hat{x}\partial_y-\hat{y}\partial_x\right)\psi(z)\\
&=\hat{t}\cdot\left(\hat{y}\partial_y+\hat{x}\partial_x\right)\psi(z)\\
&=\hat{t}\cdot\nabla\psi(z),
\end{aligned}\end{equation}
where $\hat{t}$ is the unit vector tangent to $C$ at $z$. Thus the scalar
product in \equatref{eq:LYarea} can be identified as the directional derivative
of the imaginary part along $C$, i.e. we find
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lineDensityMu}
\mu(s)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\dv{}{s}\left(\psi_2(z^+)-\psi_1(z^-)\right).
\end{equation}

Equation \eqref{eq:lineDensityMu} will let us learn how the density of zeros
changes as we approach $z_0$ along the phase boundary. Now, we want to learn
about the direction of the phase boudary near $z_0$. Suppose in the
thermodynamic limit there is a phase transition at $z_0$. Then on either side of
the phase boundary, the free energy is analytic, and thus admits an expansion
\begin{equation}
f_{1,2}\left(\tilde{z}\right)=f(z_0)+b_{1,2}\tilde{z}+c_{1,2}\tilde{z}^2+\order{\tilde{z}^3},
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{z}\equiv z-z_0$, $f_1$ holds for $\Re\tilde{z}<0$ and $f_2$ holds
for $\Re\tilde{z}>0$. For $f$ to be real along the real axis, it must be that
the coefficients $b_i$ and $c_i$ are real. As we pointed out earlier, the
potential, i.e. the real part of $f$, is continuous across the phase boundary,
which enforces $\Forall z\in C$ that $\Re f_1(z)=\Re f_2(z)$. After some
algebra, we learn from this condition that points along $C$ satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zerosLine}
\tilde{y}^2\approx\frac{b_2-b_1}{c_2-c_1}\tilde{x}+\tilde{x}^2.
\end{equation}
Thus the shape and density of $C$ depends on the order of the phase transition: 
\begin{enumerate}
\item For first-order transitions, there must be a discontinuity after one
derivative of $f$ w.r.t. the control parameter, so $b_1\neq b_2$,
and from \equatref{eq:zerosLine}, we learn the curve
is shaped like a hyperbola passing smoothly through $z_0$. Therefore the tangent
to $C$ is parallel to the imaginary axis at $z_0$, and from
\equatref{eq:lineDensityMu} we see
$$
\mu(0)=\frac{1}{2\pi}(b_2-b_1),
$$ 
i.e. the density of zeros is nonzero at $z_0$ for a first-order transition.
\item For a second-order phase transition (in the Ehrenfest sense), 
the first derivative exists, and
$b_1=b_2$. Then the first term in \equatref{eq:zerosLine} drops out, and we
learn $C$ looks linear near $z_0$. As we argued earlier, zeros of $\grandZ$
occur in complex-conjugate pairs, so we expect two lines
$\tilde{y}=\pm\tilde{x}$ passing through $z_0$. Taking the solution
$\tilde{x}=\tilde{y}=s/\sqrt{2}$ and plugging it into
\equatref{eq:lineDensityMu} we learn
$$
\mu(s)=\frac{c_2-c_1}{\pi}s,
$$
i.e. the density decreases linearly to zero as one approaches $z_0$ along $C$.
\item For a general continuous phase transition, the first nonzero term in the
Taylor expansion of $f$ will control the density. If the difference 
goes like $f_2(z)-f_1(z)\sim z^\alpha$, then the density behaves like 
$s^{\alpha-1}$ near $z_0$, i.e. for a finite density of zeros one 
needs $\alpha\geq 1$. The approach is at an angle $\pi/2\alpha$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Lee-Yang edges}

The logic of the previous section applies to the critical point $T=\Tc$ and $h=0$.
When we are away from the critical point, the situation in the complex plane
changes somewhat. Still in the thermodynamic limit, let us 
imagine\footnote{In case you forget which way the inequality goes, just
remember \figref{fig:phase_ising}. High temperatures must destroy
magnetization, so of course there's no symmetry breaking there.} $T>\Tc$ 
and let $h$ be complex. The expectation is that $\log Z(h)$ should
have two branch cuts in the complex-$h$ plane, called 
{\it Lee-Yang edges}\index{Lee-Yang!edge} (LYE), that terminate at
branch points called {\it LYE singularities}~\cite{lee_statistical_1952}.
This is shown schematically in \figref{fig:LYE}. According to Lee and Yang, 
as $T\to\Tc$, these branch points will approach and eventually
pinch the real axis, where they manifest as a phase transition
in accordance with the Lee-Yang theorem.
Moreover, LYE singularities can be regarded as critical points in their
own right, in the sense that as one approaches the LYE singularities 
in the complex $h$ plane, the order parameter will diverge
according to the dimension and critical 
exponents~\cite{fisher_yang-lee_1978}. In particular $M\sim h^\sigma$, where
\begin{equation}
\sigma=\frac{1}{\delta}=\frac{d-2+\eta}{d+2-\eta}.
\end{equation}
What is remarkable about these edges is that, generally speaking,
there is no expectation on how complex singularities of a function
should organize themselves; Lee and Yang show that for the Ising model,
the lie exactly on the imaginary $h$ axis.
As shown in \figref{fig:LYE}, the LYEs should limit the convergence
radius of $\log Z$. 
%In principle there are other singularities possible.
%The {\it extended analyticity conjecture}\index{extended analyticity conjecture}
%posits that the Lee-Yang edges should be the closest singularities
%to the origin in the complex $h$-plane~\cite{fonseca_ising_2003}. 

For a finite system there will be no zeros of the partition function
on the real axis, but there will generically be zeroes in the complex
plane. As the system size grows, these zeros coalesce and form
branch cuts of $\log Z$ in the thermodynamic limit.
There is an intuition that if these cuts pinch the real axis we have a
second-order transition, while if they cross it there is a first-order.
One way to think about this latter case is that for first-order transitions, we
get these discontinuous jumps in the order parameter. That matches what you
would expect to happen for a generic complex function as you cross a branch cut:
you jump discontinuously from one Riemann sheet to the next.

\section{RG transformations and flow}\label{sec:RGflow}\index{renormalization group}

We first start with a heuristic introduction to the 
renormalization group (RG).
Here, we want to discuss in broad strokes what it does and
why it is useful. For this introduction, we
will follow Wilson and Kogut's review article about the renormalization group,
which is in my opinion one of the best introductions on the topic there
is~\cite{wilson_renormalization_1974}.

Most systems that we study have a huge number of degrees of freedom. A chunk of
material has $\order{10^{23}}$ molecules. In quantum field theory, a field
$\phi$ at a spacetime point $x$ is allowed to take many possible values, so even
in arbitrarily small regions of spacetime there are uncountably infinitely many
degrees of freedom. When modeling, we generally don't consider every degree of
freedom and try to make assumptions to simplify calculations so they reduce to
just a handful. A common strategy is to represent large numbers of degrees of
freedom with some kind of average over them. For example in hydrodynamics, one
introduces a new quantity, the density $\rho$, rather than dealing with the
mass of each individual particle. This simplification works when we're
interested in macroscopic/long-distance behavior, where fluctuations in the
density at short distances can be safely ignored.\footnote{Essentially what we are doing
is ``zooming out" or ``decreasing resolution" until our modeling becomes
tractable. An interesting question in this context is: How far do I have to
``zoom out" before fluctuations at shorter scales become uncorrelated and can be
averaged away? The length scale that controls the boundary where this
qualitative change happens is the correlation length\index{correlation length}
$\xi$. Near a critical point $\xi$ grows large, and naive averaging fails.}

The RG attempts to formalize this. There are different ways to reduce the
degrees of freedom. In the context of spin models for magnets, one can average
spins over small boxes, constructing new, effective spins. Or one can simply
remove some fraction of the spins, e.g. removing half of them by removing the
spin on every even-parity site. Let's call this spin-reduction transformation
$R$. Let's say our system starts with Hamiltonian $H^{(0)}$, and we assume it
contains only local interactions, i.e. the interaction range between spins is
$L_0$. Assume also we're away from a critical point. The effective Hamiltonian I
am left with after one RG transformation is
\begin{equation}
H^{(1)}=RH^{(0)}.
\end{equation}
The hope is that the interaction range of $H^{(1)}$ is now something larger, say
$bL_0$ with $b>1$. Repeated applications of the transformation yield
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RGiterativeHamiltonian}
H^{(n)}=R^nH^{(0)}.
\end{equation}
Our ``zooming out" process $R$ can be almost anything we can imagine, provided
$H^{(n)}$ has the ``same form" as $H^{(0)}$. We will make this idea more precise
later. $H^{(n)}$ should be easier to work with than $H^{(0)}$, and that can be a
nice use of the RG.

But perhaps for us, the more interesting application is this: We want to
understand how systems with differing microscopic details can lead to the same
macroscopic behavior. For instance, we can try to understand how different
systems can belong to the same universality class. Systems in the same
universality class exhibit the same kinds of cooperative behavior regardless of
what the system looks like when you zoom in. The RG proffers an
explanation for this phenomenon. For consider what could happen in
\equatref{eq:RGiterativeHamiltonian} after applying infinitely many
transformations, i.e. after zooming out as much as possible. 
The simplest thing that could happen would be 
to encounter a {\it fixed point}\index{fixed point}, i.e. a Hamiltonian $H^*$ 
satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RGfixedPoint}
H^*=RH^*.
\end{equation}
The situations we will study all exhibit fixed points, and crucially, note that
\equatref{eq:RGfixedPoint} {\it makes no reference to the original Hamiltonian
$H^{(0)}$ at all}; in short, it doesn't care about the microscopic
details. A system with multiple fixed points has multiple kinds of different
cooperative behavior, and depending on where in parameter space $H^{(0)}$
starts, one might end up at any of them after successive RG transformations. 

As we will see, phases correspond to attractive fixed
points, each of which pulls in the couplings from a whole region of parameter
space, while transitions are controlled by fixed points with relevant directions
sitting on the boundaries between such regions. Not every fixed point is a phase.
The kind of fixed point controlling a boundary determines the kind of transition,
whether first or second order. And if no such boundary passes through the physical parameter
space, there is no transition at all, as we will see for the \dimens{1} Ising model.
The same logic underlies the dependence of lattice field theories on the lattice
spacing, discussed around \equatref{eq:RGflow}.

In this section we set up RG transformations, work through the \dimens{1} Ising
model as an example, and discuss the general structure of the resulting
{\it RG flow}. In \secref{sec:scalingHypothesis} we specialize to the
neighborhood of a fixed point, where we derive relations among the critical
exponents of second-order transitions and treat first-order transitions.

\subsection{Block-spin transformations}

Suppose we start with a system of variables $\set{\sigma}$ with Hamiltonian $H$
and $n$ couplings $\vec{k}=(k_1,...,k_n)^t$. After one RG step, we obtain
a new system of spins $\set{\sigma'}$ with $H'$ and $\vec{k}'$.
It is common to refer to $\set{\sigma}$ as the {\it fine}\index{fine}
lattice and $\set{\sigma'}$ as the {\it coarse}\index{coarse} lattice.
If each coarse spin replaces a block of $b^d$ fine spins, the lattice spacing
grows by a factor $b$, which is called the {\it scale factor}\index{scale factor}.
We want
\begin{itemize}
\item expectation values to remain unchanged after an RG step; and
\item $H'$ has the same functional form as $H$.
\end{itemize}

Let's take a moment to discuss what the second point means. The original Hamiltonian can be
written like
\begin{equation}
H\left(\{\sigma\},\vec{k}\right)=\sum_a k_a S_a\left(\{\sigma\}\right).
\end{equation}
Each $k_a$ is a component of the coupling vector, and $S_a$ is a rule that
specifies the interaction type. For example one possibility is nearest-neighbor
interactions, so we could write
\begin{equation}
S_1=\sum_{\ev{ij}}\sigma_i\sigma_j,
\end{equation}
and e.g. in the Ising model, we would have $k_1=-J$. An external coupling would
be
\begin{equation}
S_2=\sum_{i}\sigma_i,
\end{equation}
and thus $k_2=h$. The statement that $H'$ has the same functional form means
\begin{equation}
H'\left(\{\sigma'\},\vec{k'}\right)=\sum_a k'_a S_a\left(\{\sigma'\}\right).
\end{equation}
For this to make sense, at least three things must be true of the RG transform:
\begin{enumerate}
\item We need the same kind of spins. In particular if the fine spins can only
take the values $\pm1$ like in the Ising model, so must the coarse spins.
\item The geometry of the coarse and fine lattices needs to be the same. So e.g.
an RG transformation of a hypercubic lattice should yield a hypercubic lattice.
\item The rules $S_a$ need to run over all possible interactions that can be
introduced in the RG step; i.e. the set of couplings should be closed. My
original $H$ may have only nearest-neighbor terms, so $k_1=-J$ and $k_i=0$
otherwise. Generally speaking, the RG transform will introduce new interactions
between the spins.
\end{enumerate}
Over the course of this section and the next, we are going to try to understand
which of these many couplings actually matter.

Thinking about block transformations in particular, one can
ensure that expectation values remain unchanged by collecting
all those original configurations $\set{\sigma}$ that lead to the same
renormalized configuration $\set{\sigma'}$\footnote{For example in the \dimens{2}
Ising model, if one blocks three up-spins and one down spin, there are
four possible ways to get the same effective spin via majority rule.}, which can be
summarized as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RGxformBoltzmann}
  e^{-H'\left(\set{\sigma'},~\vec{k}'\right)}
     =e^{-\beta G(\vec{k})}\sum_{\set{\sigma}~
                        {\rm giving}~\set{\sigma'}}
      e^{-H\left(\set{\sigma},~\vec{k}\right)}.
\end{equation}
Here we have expressed an overall normalization as $e^{-\beta G}$, which will
be convenient later. The form makes sense: each exponential is proportional to a
probability, so the probability of getting the coarse configuration $\{\sigma'\}$ is
just the sum over all the probabilities of each fine configuration $\{\sigma\}$
leading to it. In other words, we marginalize over $\{\sigma\}$.

Assuming $H'$ has the same functional form as $H$, the outcome of an RG step is
completely characterized by the new couplings. Hence an RG step defines a map
in the space of couplings,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RGmap}
  \vec{k}'=\vec{R}(\vec{k}),
\end{equation}
which we write as $\vec{R}_b$ when we want to emphasize the scale factor.
The normalization $G$ does not enter $\vec{R}$; it is a constant term in $H'$
that we have split off. We come back to it in \secref{sec:RGfreeEnergy}.

\subsection{Example: the \dimens{1} Ising model}\label{sec:1dIsingRG}

Consider the \dimens{1} Ising chain with
\begin{equation}
  H=-J\sum_i \sigma_i\sigma_{i+1}-h\sum_i \sigma_i,
\end{equation}
which is \equatref{eq:ising} in \dimens{1}. As in \equatref{eq:RGxformBoltzmann}, the
Boltzmann weight is $e^{-H}$, i.e. the factor of $\beta$ has been absorbed into
$J$ and $h$, so that $J>0$ for a ferromagnet. The simplest RG transformation is
{\it decimation}\index{decimation}: we sum over every other spin, say those on
even sites, and keep the spins on odd sites unchanged as the coarse spins.
Hence $b=2$. In terms of \equatref{eq:RGxformBoltzmann}, ``$\set{\sigma}$ giving
$\set{\sigma'}$" just means that the odd spins of $\set{\sigma}$ equal
$\set{\sigma'}$.

Start with $h=0$. Each even spin, say $\sigma_2$, appears only in the bonds to its
neighbors $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_3$, so summing over it gives
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\sigma_2=\pm1}e^{J\sigma_2(\sigma_1+\sigma_3)}=2\cosh J(\sigma_1+\sigma_3).
\end{equation}
We would like to write this as $A\,e^{J'\sigma_1\sigma_3}$, i.e. a nearest-neighbor
interaction on the coarse lattice times a constant. Since $\sigma_1\sigma_3=\pm1$, there
are only two cases to match:
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
  \sigma_1=\sigma_3:&~~~~2\cosh2J=Ae^{J'},\\
  \sigma_1=-\sigma_3:&~~~~2=Ae^{-J'},
\end{aligned}\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:1dIsingRG}
  e^{2J'}=\cosh2J,~~~~~~A=2\sqrt{\cosh2J}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $\tanh J'=\tanh^2J$. The coarse Hamiltonian has exactly the same
form as the fine one, only with a new coupling $J'$. The constant $A$ cannot be
absorbed into $J'$. There is one factor of $A$ for each of the $N/2$ decimated
spins, so altogether we pick up $A^{N/2}=\exp[(N/2)\log A]$. This is the
normalization $e^{-\beta G}$ of \equatref{eq:RGxformBoltzmann}, and we see
explicitly that $G$ is extensive.

With $h\neq0$ the same procedure, now matching the three cases $\sigma_1=\sigma_3=1$,
$\sigma_1=\sigma_3=-1$, and $\sigma_1=-\sigma_3$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:1dIsingRGh}
  e^{4J'}=\frac{\cosh(2J+h)\cosh(2J-h)}{\cosh^2h},~~~~~~
  h'=h+\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{\cosh(2J+h)}{\cosh(2J-h)}.
\end{equation}
The correction to $h$ arises because each coarse spin neighbors two decimated
spins, each of which leaves behind a contribution to its field.

That the coarse Hamiltonian closes on itself is special to \dimens{1}. Consider
the square lattice at $h=0$, and decimate one sublattice of the checkerboard,
so that the remaining spins form a square lattice rotated by $45^\circ$
with $b=\sqrt{2}$. Each decimated spin now has four neighbors, and summing
over it gives $2\cosh J(\sigma_1+\sigma_2+\sigma_3+\sigma_4)$. This takes three distinct values
depending on whether $\sigma_1+\sigma_2+\sigma_3+\sigma_4$ is $\pm4$, $\pm2$, or 0, which cannot be
matched by a constant and a nearest-neighbor coupling alone. Instead one finds
a nearest-neighbor coupling $J_1$, a next-nearest-neighbor coupling $J_2$, and a
four-spin plaquette coupling $J_4$ given by
\begin{equation}
  J_1=\frac{1}{4}\log\cosh4J,~~~~J_2=\frac{1}{8}\log\cosh4J,~~~~
  J_4=\frac{1}{8}\log\cosh4J-\frac{1}{2}\log\cosh2J.
\end{equation}
Further RG steps generate ever longer-ranged and higher-order couplings. Hence
in general $\vec{k}$ lives in an infinite-dimensional space of all interactions
allowed by the symmetries, and the original model is a point in this space where
most couplings vanish. In practice the generated couplings fall off quickly with
range, so that truncating to a handful of short-range couplings works well.
Majority-rule transformations also generate new couplings. Moreover the sum over
$\set{\sigma}$ no longer factorizes, since spins in neighboring blocks are coupled,
so $H'$ cannot be computed in closed form. In that case the renormalized couplings
can be determined numerically, e.g. with 
Monte Carlo RG~\cite{ma_renormalization_1976,swendsen_monte_1979}.

\subsection{RG flow and fixed points}\label{sec:fixedPoints}

Applying the RG transformation repeatedly yields a sequence of couplings
\begin{equation}
  \vec{k}^{(0)}=\vec{k},~~~~\vec{k}^{(1)}=\vec{R}(\vec{k}),~~~~
  \vec{k}^{(2)}=\vec{R}\big(\vec{R}(\vec{k})\big),~~~~...
\end{equation}
called a {\it trajectory}\index{trajectory}. Each point of the trajectory
describes the same long-distance physics, viewed at successively coarser
resolution: after $n$ steps, a coarse spin represents a block of linear
extension $b^n$. The collection of all trajectories is called the
{\it RG flow}\index{RG flow}, and it is often drawn as arrows in the space of
couplings, a so-called {\it flow diagram}\index{flow diagram}. A
{\it fixed point}\index{fixed point} $\vec{k}_0$ is a point that does not
move, $\vec{R}(\vec{k}_0)=\vec{k}_0$.

At this point, it is worth pointing out that fixed points do not necessarily
exist. In practice, it is common to assume the existence of a fixed point and
check for consistency. This consistency could include, e.g. agreement with
experiment or with exact solutions, when they exist. In some special cases,
existence of fixed points is known rigorously. For these notes, we will take the
approach where we simply assume existence.

Fixed points have a simple characterization in terms of the
{\it correlation length}\index{correlation length} $\xi$. This is defined at any
point in coupling space, in any phase, through the long-distance decay of the
connected correlation function,
\begin{equation}
  \ev{\sigma_0\sigma_r}-\ev{\sigma_0}\ev{\sigma_r}\sim e^{-r/\xi}
  ~~~~\text{as}~~~~r\to\infty,
\end{equation}
where $r$ is measured in units of the lattice spacing. Since the RG step shrinks
all lengths by $b$ while leaving long-distance correlations unchanged,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RGxi}
  \xi(\vec{k}')=\frac{\xi(\vec{k})}{b}
\end{equation}
everywhere in coupling space, not just near a transition. At a fixed point this
reads $\xi=\xi/b$, so either $\xi=0$ or $\xi=\infty$. Fixed points with $\xi=0$
are called {\it trivial}. Fixed points with $\xi=\infty$ are called
{\it critical}; these are scale invariant in a nontrivial sense, with
fluctuations on all length scales.

\equatref{eq:RGxi} also tells us where most trajectories go. If $\xi$ is
finite, then after $n$ steps $\xi^{(n)}=\xi/b^n\to0$, so the trajectory must end
at a trivial fixed point. This covers the bulk of every phase, such as the
paramagnet flowing to the uncorrelated $T=\infty$ fixed point, and it also covers
first-order transitions, where $\xi$ stays finite; we will see in
\secref{sec:scalingHypothesis} that these are controlled by trivial fixed points
with a relevant direction. Only points with $\xi=\infty$ can flow into a critical
fixed point. Second-order transitions are therefore the special case where $\xi$
diverges.

Let's see how this works in the \dimens{1} Ising model at $h=0$. Introducing
$u\equiv\tanh J$, which runs from 0 at $T=\infty$ to 1 at $T=0$ since $\beta$
was absorbed in the coupling,
\equatref{eq:1dIsingRG} becomes
\begin{equation}
  u'=u^2,
\end{equation}
so the trajectory starting from $u$ is $u^{(n)}=u^{2^n}$. There are two
fixed points, $u=0$ ($J=0$, $T=\infty$) and $u=1$ ($J=\infty$, $T=0$). One can
check \equatref{eq:RGxi} explicitly: the correlation function of the \dimens{1} Ising
chain is $\ev{\sigma_0\sigma_r}=u^r$, which is also the connected correlation
function, since $\ev{\sigma_i}=0$ at $h=0$ for all $T>0$.\footnote{By symmetry
$\ev{\sigma_i}=0$ in any finite chain at $h=0$, but that alone doesn't rule out
spontaneous magnetization, which requires taking $N\to\infty$ before $h\to0$.
The transfer matrix gives, in the thermodynamic limit,
$m(h)=\sinh h/\sqrt{\sinh^2h+e^{-4J}}$, which is analytic in $h$ and vanishes at
$h=0$ for any finite $J$. Only at $J=\infty$ does it become
$m=\operatorname{sign}(h)$. Equivalently, spontaneous order would require
$\ev{\sigma_0\sigma_r}\to m^2$ as $r\to\infty$, while $u^r\to0$ for $u<1$.
At $u=1$ itself $\ev{\sigma_0\sigma_r}=1$ for all $r$, and the connected
correlator depends on which state one picks, so the statement that $u=1$ has
$\xi=\infty$ should be understood as the limit $u\to1^-$.}
Hence $\xi=-1/\log u$, and indeed
$\xi(u^2)=\xi(u)/2$. The fixed point $u=0$ has $\xi=0$, while $u=1$ has
$\xi=\infty$; in \dimens{1}, $T=0$ is a critical point.

To figure out whether a fixed point $u_0$ attracts or repels nearby points, we
linearize the flow around it. We measure both the starting coupling and its
image after one RG step from the fixed point, writing
\begin{equation}
  u=u_0+\delta u,~~~~~~u'=u_0+\delta u',
\end{equation}
so that $\delta u'$ is the deviation from $u_0$ after the step. Plugging these
into $u'=u^2$ gives
\begin{equation}
  u_0+\delta u'=(u_0+\delta u)^2=u_0^2+2u_0\,\delta u+(\delta u)^2.
\end{equation}
Since $u_0$ is a fixed point, $u_0^2=u_0$, so the $u_0$ on each side cancels,
leaving $\delta u'=2u_0\,\delta u+(\delta u)^2$. For small deviations we drop
the quadratic term to find
\begin{equation}
  \delta u'=2u_0\,\delta u.
\end{equation}
More generally, for a recursion $u'=R(u)$ with $R(u_0)=u_0$, Taylor expanding
$R(u_0+\delta u)$ gives $\delta u'=R'(u_0)\,\delta u$, so the factor $2u_0$
is just $R'(u_0)$. We will generalize this to many couplings in
\secref{sec:scalingFields}.
At $u_0=0$ the factor is $0<1$, so small deviations shrink: the fixed point
is {\it attractive}, or {\it stable}. At $u_0=1$ the factor is $2>1$, so small
deviations grow: the fixed point is {\it repulsive}, or {\it unstable}. Every
starting point with $u<1$, i.e. every $T>0$, flows to $u=0$. Hence at any
nonzero temperature, the long-distance physics of the \dimens{1} Ising chain is that
of uncorrelated spins at $T=\infty$, i.e. the chain is always paramagnetic.

Near the unstable fixed point it is convenient to use $w\equiv1-u\approx2e^{-2J}$,
which measures the distance from $T=0$. Then $w'=1-(1-w)^2\approx2w$, and
$\xi=-1/\log(1-w)\approx1/w$, which diverges like $e^{2J}/2$ as $T\to0$. We can
also look at the field. As $J\to\infty$ at fixed $h$, the ratio of
$\cosh$'s in \equatref{eq:1dIsingRGh} approaches $e^{2h}$, so that $h'=2h$.
Hence the $T=0$ fixed point has two unstable directions, $w$ and $h$, each
with factor $2$. Meanwhile at $J=0$, \equatref{eq:1dIsingRGh} gives $h'=h$, so
the $T=\infty$ fixed point is really a line of fixed points parameterized by $h$,
describing free spins in a field.

\subsection{Linearization and scaling fields}\label{sec:scalingFields}

Next, let us think about how to relate the couplings $\vec{k}'$ and $\vec{k}$
near a general fixed point $\vec{k}_0$.
We can Taylor expand the renormalization transform $\vec{R}$ in the vicinity of $\vec{k}_0$ to find
\begin{equation}
  \vec{k}_0+\delta\vec{k}'=\vec{R}\left(\vec{k}_0+\delta\vec{k}\right)
                          \approx\vec{R}(\vec{k}_0)
                           +\left[\frac{\partial R_i(\vec{k})}{\partial k_j}
                              \right]_{\vec{k}=\vec{k}_0}\delta\vec{k}.
\end{equation}
The first-order term defines a matrix $M$; it's the Jacobian.
Since the effect of the renormalization transform
$\vec{R}$ on the fixed point is $\vec{R}(\vec{k}_0)=\vec{k}_0$, we can write
\begin{equation}
  \delta\vec{k}'=M\delta\vec{k}.
\end{equation}
Now let us switch to a basis in which $M$ is diagonal.\footnote{In general $M$ is
not symmetric, so it is an assumption that it can be diagonalized with real
eigenvalues. This assumption is standard and appears to hold in practice.}
In this basis, our original system contains new couplings $\vec{x}$, and hence
the renormalized couplings $\vec{x}'$ are given according to the eigenvalues of
$M$ as
\begin{equation}
  x_i'=\lambda_i x_i~~~~\text{no summation}.
\end{equation}
The $x_i$ are called {\it scaling fields}\index{scaling field}.

The eigenvalues depend on the scale factor, $\lambda_i=\lambda_i(b)$.
Two RG steps with scale factors $b_1$ and $b_2$ amount to one step with
scale factor $b_1b_2$, so the linearized transformations satisfy
$M(b_1)M(b_2)=M(b_1b_2)$. These matrices commute, so they share eigenvectors,
and hence
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_i(b_1)\lambda_i(b_2)=\lambda_i(b_1b_2).
\end{equation}
The only continuous solution to this is a power law, so we can write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RGeigenvalue}
  \lambda_i=b^{\,y_i},
\end{equation}
where the exponent $y_i$ is independent of $b$.
(This also shows the eigenvalues are positive, since
$\lambda_i(b)=\lambda_i(\sqrt{b})^2$.) Strictly speaking, a blocking
procedure only composes for particular values of $b$, such as powers of 2 for
$2\times2$ blocks, and treating $b$ as continuous is an idealization.

The exponents $y_i$ classify the directions around the fixed point:
\begin{itemize}
\item $y_i>0$: the scaling field grows under the RG. These are called
{\it relevant}\index{relevant}.
\item $y_i<0$: the scaling field shrinks under the RG. These are called
{\it irrelevant}\index{irrelevant}.
\item $y_i=0$: the linear analysis is inconclusive, and one has to go to higher
order. These are called {\it marginal}\index{marginal}, and they typically lead
to logarithmic corrections.
\end{itemize}
In the \dimens{1} Ising model, both $w$ and $h$ have $\lambda=2=b^1$ at the $T=0$
fixed point, so they are relevant with $y=1$. Note that whether a coupling is
relevant depends on the fixed point we are expanding around.

The set of all points that flow into $\vec{k}_0$ is called its
{\it critical surface}\index{critical surface}. This includes $\vec{k}_0$ itself,
which trivially flows into itself. Near $\vec{k}_0$ we can characterize the
surface with the scaling fields. After $n$ steps, $x_i^{(n)}=b^{ny_i}x_i$, so
irrelevant components shrink to zero while any nonzero relevant component
grows. A point therefore flows into $\vec{k}_0$ only if all of its relevant
components vanish, i.e. near $\vec{k}_0$ the critical surface is
\begin{equation}
  x_i=0~~~~\text{for all relevant}~i,
\end{equation}
and it is spanned by the irrelevant directions. Moving along an irrelevant
direction keeps us on the surface, and the flow carries us back toward
$\vec{k}_0$, which is the attractor within the surface. The relevant directions
point off the surface. A point slightly off the surface first approaches
$\vec{k}_0$ as its irrelevant components die out, then gets pushed away along
the relevant directions toward some other fixed point. Hence $\vec{k}_0$ is a
saddle point of the flow. Farther from $\vec{k}_0$ the surface is curved, and
the condition $x_i=0$ should be replaced by the vanishing of the nonlinear
scaling fields discussed in \secref{sec:scalingHypothesis}.

Note that the RG flow is a separate thing from choosing a starting point. A
given microscopic model does not explore all of coupling space; as we vary its
physical parameters, such as $T$ and $h$, its couplings trace out a
low-dimensional family $\vec{k}(T,h,...)$. To put the model on the critical
surface, we have to tune these parameters so that
\begin{equation}
  x_i\big(\vec{k}(T,h,...)\big)=0~~~~\text{for all relevant}~i.
\end{equation}
This is one condition per relevant direction, so the number of relevant
directions is the number of parameters one has to tune. For the Ising
model in $d\geq2$ one has to tune both $T$ and $h$, and indeed the critical
fixed point has exactly two relevant directions. Symmetry helps to identify
them: since the RG transformation respects the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ symmetry, $M$ does
not mix $\mathbb{Z}_2$-even and $\mathbb{Z}_2$-odd perturbations. Hence one
relevant direction is temperature-like and the other is field-like. All other
directions, of which there are infinitely many, are irrelevant.

This is the origin of {\it universality}\index{universality}. Different
microscopic models that lie on the same critical surface all flow to the same
$\vec{k}_0$, and their differences, which lie along irrelevant directions, die out
under the flow.

Finally, the scaling fields determine how the correlation length behaves near
$\vec{k}_0$. Consider a single relevant field $x$ with exponent $y$. By
\equatref{eq:RGxi}, $\xi(b^{\,y}x)=\xi(x)/b$. Since this holds for any $b$, we
can choose $b=|x|^{-1/y}$ to find
\begin{equation}
  \xi(x)=|x|^{-1/y}\,\xi(\pm1).
\end{equation}
In particular, identifying $x$ with the reduced temperature $t$, we find
$\nu=1/y_t$. In the \dimens{1} Ising model this gives $\xi\sim w^{-1}$, which agrees
with the exact result above.

\subsection{The free energy and the role of fixed points}\label{sec:RGfreeEnergy}

When we sum \equatref{eq:RGxformBoltzmann} over all $\set{\sigma'}$ we get
\begin{equation}
  Z'(\vec{k}')=e^{-\beta G(\vec{k})} Z(\vec{k}),
\end{equation}
or in terms of free energies,
\begin{equation}
  F'(\vec{k}')=F(\vec{k})+G(\vec{k}).
\end{equation}
Now $F$ is extensive, so we can write
\begin{equation}
  F(\vec{k})=Nf(\vec{k})
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of spins on the original lattice and $f$ is intensive.
Strictly speaking this is only true in the thermodynamic limit; on a finite
lattice there are corrections from surface terms and other finite-size effects.
We therefore define
\begin{equation}
  f(\vec{k})\equiv\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{F_N(\vec{k})}{N},
\end{equation}
so that from here on, $f$ and $g$ are statements about the infinite system.
Similarly one can write
\begin{equation}
  F'(\vec{k}')=\frac{N}{b^d}f(\vec{k}')~~~~\text{and}~~~~G(\vec{k})=Ng(\vec{k}),
\end{equation}
since the coarse lattice has $N/b^d$ spins. That $F$ and $F'$ are proportional
to the same intensive function with different couplings follows from the fact
that the effective Hamiltonian $H'$ has the same functional form as $H$.
Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:widom1}
f(\vec{k}')=b^d\left(f(\vec{k})+g(\vec{k})\right).
\end{equation}

Before going further, it is worth asking where the non-analyticity of a phase
transition can possibly come from. Iterating \equatref{eq:widom1} along the
trajectory $\vec{k}^{(n)}$, we find
\begin{equation}
  f(\vec{k})=-\sum_{n=0}^\infty b^{-nd}\,g\left(\vec{k}^{(n)}\right).
\end{equation}
Each $g$ comes from summing over the finitely many spins in a block, so it is
analytic in the couplings, and so is each individual RG step. Any singular
behavior of $f$ must therefore come from the infinite sum, i.e. from the
behavior of the trajectory $\vec{k}^{(n)}$ as $n\to\infty$. In typical cases,
trajectories end up at fixed points.

The existence of fixed points alone is not enough, though. Every model has
fixed points, including models without a phase transition. What we need is for
the long-time behavior of $\vec{k}^{(n)}$ to depend non-analytically on the
starting couplings $\vec{k}$. If all trajectories near some $\vec{k}$ run into the same
attractive fixed point, then nearby starting points have nearby trajectories,
and $f$ is analytic there. This is the situation deep inside a phase.
A singularity requires a fixed point with at least one relevant direction
sitting on the boundary between the basins of attraction of different
attractive fixed points. Starting points on either side of this boundary,
however close, eventually flow to different places.

To see how this produces a power law, suppose we start near such a fixed point,
with a small relevant component $u$ having exponent $y>0$. This
component grows like $b^{\,ny}u$, so the trajectory lingers near $\vec{k}_0$ for
about
\begin{equation}
  n^*\approx-\frac{\log_b|u|}{y}
\end{equation}
steps before escaping. Stopping the iteration of \equatref{eq:widom1} at $n^*$,
the singular part is $b^{-n^*d}$ times something of order one, i.e.
\begin{equation}
  \fsing\sim b^{-n^*d}=|u|^{d/y}.
\end{equation}
The non-analyticity comes from the number of steps $n^*$ depending on
$\log|u|$, which diverges at $u=0$.

The \dimens{1} Ising model of \secref{sec:1dIsingRG} illustrates this. Its only
fixed point with relevant directions is at $T=0$, at the edge of the physical
parameter space. Every $T>0$ flows to the attractive fixed point at $T=\infty$,
so $f$ is analytic for all $T>0$, and the only singularity is at $T=0$, where
$\xi\sim e^{2J}$ diverges. Hence the correct statement is that we seek fixed
points with relevant directions whose critical surface intersects the physical
parameter space. Near such fixed points, the RG map also becomes simple enough
to solve \equatref{eq:widom1}, which we do in \secref{sec:scalingHypothesis}.
Note that the physical system we started with, e.g. the nearest-neighbor
Ising model at $\Tc$, is generally {\it not} at $\vec{k}_0$; it merely flows there.

This picture is consistent with the Lee-Yang result of \secref{sec:leeyang}.
On a lattice of linear extension $L$, you can only carry out about $\log_b L$
blocking steps before running out of lattice. The sum above then contains finitely
many terms, each analytic, so $f$ is analytic and there is no phase transition.
The sum only becomes infinite as $L\to\infty$; the thermodynamic limit and the
infinite iteration of the RG are one and the same limit.

\section{The scaling hypothesis}\label{sec:scalingHypothesis}

All critical exponents\index{critical exponent} can be written as functions
of the number of dimensions $d$ as well as $\nu$ and $\eta$. Given the reduced
temperature\index{temperature!reduced}
\begin{equation}
  t\equiv\frac{T-T_c}{T_c}
\end{equation}
and external magnetic field $h$ one gets the
relationships~\tabref{tab:scaling}.
These are equivalent to the {\it hyperscaling relations}
\index{hyperscaling relation}
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
2\beta+\gamma&=2-\alpha,\\
2\beta\delta-\gamma&=2-\alpha,\\
\gamma&=\nu(2-\eta),\\
\nu d&=2-\alpha.
\end{aligned}\end{equation}
These can be derived from the {\it scaling hypothesis}\index{scaling hypothesis},
which we will now derive using the RG machinery of \secref{sec:RGflow}.
\begin{table}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{lCr} \hline\hline
       Exponent & Definition & Value\\[3pt]\hline
$\alpha$ & $C_V\sim|t|^{-\alpha}$  & $2-\nu d$\\[3pt]
$\beta$ & $m\sim|t|^{\beta}$ & $\frac{1}{2}\nu(d+\eta-2)$\\[3pt]
$\gamma$ & $\chi\sim|t|^{-\gamma}$  & $\nu(2-\eta)$\\[3pt]
$\delta$ & $m\sim h^{1/\delta}$ &  $\frac{d+2-\eta}{d-2+\eta}$\\[3pt]
$\nu$ & $\xi\sim |t|^{-\nu}$ & \\[3pt]
        \hline\hline
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Relationships among the critical exponents. Table adapted
         from Ref.~\cite{binney_theory_1992}. In each case one coupling is
         small while the other is fixed to zero. For $\beta$, $t$ approaches
         zero from below.}
\label{tab:scaling}
\end{table}

We work near a critical fixed point $\vec{k}_0$, in terms of the scaling fields
$\vec{x}$ of \secref{sec:scalingFields}, which transform as
$x_i'=b^{\,y_i}x_i$. Hence we can recast \equatref{eq:widom1} as
\begin{equation}
f(b^{\,y_1}x_1,b^{\,y_2}x_2,...)=b^d\left(f(x_1,x_2,...)+g(x_1,x_2,...)\right).
\end{equation}

Now $f$ and $g$ are some kinds of functions of $\vec{x}$. Generally speaking
there will be some part that is expressible as a Taylor series in the $\vec{x}$;
this is called the {\it regular} part.\index{regular} There must also be some
part not expressible as a Taylor series in order that we can obtain the power
law behavior one finds in the vicinity of a phase transition; this is
called the {\it singular}\index{singular} part.\footnote{In case the relevant
combination of critical exponents is rational, writing an observable as the
singular part plus the regular part leads to a {\it Puiseux
series}\index{Puiseux series}, a generalization of a power series that allows
for fractional powers.} In order to proceed we make
the following assumption: The singular part is completely contained in
$f$. This is not true of all RG transformations, but it is true
of many; for example it can be shown that this assumption holds for any
RG transformation whose renormalized block variables depend linearly
on the original ones. Under this assumption we can write
\begin{equation}
  \fsing(b^{\,y_1}x_1,b^{\,y_2}x_2,...)=b^d\fsing(x_1,x_2,...).
\end{equation}

Finally we specialize to our couplings $t$ and $h$. Sufficiently close to the
critical point, these can be identified with the two relevant scaling fields,
say $x_1=t$ and $x_2=h$.

This deserves some explanation, since being close to the critical point is not
the same as being close to the fixed point. A physical model at small $t$ and $h$
sits close to the critical surface, but it may be far from $\vec{k}_0$. The resolution
is to run the RG for a while first. Under the first $n_0$ steps, the irrelevant
components shrink like $b^{\,n_0y_i}$ with $y_i<0$, so the flow slides along the
critical surface toward $\vec{k}_0$. Meanwhile the relevant components grow, but
since they started small, they are still small after $n_0$ steps. At this point
$\vec{k}^{(n_0)}$ is close to $\vec{k}_0$ in every direction, and the linearization
is valid. Splitting the iterated sum for $f$ at $n_0$,
\begin{equation}
  f(\vec{k})=-\sum_{n<n_0} b^{-nd}\,g\left(\vec{k}^{(n)}\right)
             +b^{-n_0d}f\left(\vec{k}^{(n_0)}\right).
\end{equation}
The first term is a finite sum of analytic functions, so it only contributes
to the regular part; the singular part is decided entirely near $\vec{k}_0$.

The price is that the variables entering the scaling form are not exactly $t$
and $h$. The first $n_0$ steps are an analytic map from the physical couplings to
coordinates near $\vec{k}_0$, so the eigendirections near $\vec{k}_0$ correspond to
{\it nonlinear scaling fields}\index{scaling field!nonlinear}
\begin{equation}
  u_t(t,h)=a_1t+a_2h^2+...,~~~~~~u_h(t,h)=b_1h+b_2th+...,
\end{equation}
which are analytic in the physical couplings and vanish on the critical
surface. The constants $a_1$ and $b_1$ are non-universal; they carry the memory
of where the model started. Being ``sufficiently close" to the critical point
then means $t$ and $h$ are small enough that
\begin{enumerate}
\item the trajectory spends many steps near $\vec{k}_0$, which requires $\xi$ to be
large compared to the lattice spacing;
\item $u_t\approx a_1t$ and $u_h\approx b_1h$, otherwise one gets
{\it analytic corrections to scaling}; and
\item the remaining irrelevant components are negligible, otherwise one gets
{\it Wegner corrections}\index{corrections to scaling} of relative size
$|t|^{\omega\nu}$, where $\omega=-y_3>0$ is minus the leading irrelevant
eigenvalue exponent. In the \dimens{3} Ising model $\omega\nu\approx0.52$, so these
fade slowly and are visible in practice.
\end{enumerate}
How far the model starts from $\vec{k}_0$ along the critical surface only
affects the size of these corrections, not the exponents. This is
universality again: the model never needs to be near $\vec{k}_0$;
only its flow does.

With this identification,
\begin{equation}
  \fsing(b^{\,y_t}t,b^{\,y_h}h,...)=b^d\fsing(t,h,...).
\end{equation}
Since this holds for any $b$, we are free to choose $b$ as we like. Note that
this freedom comes from the composition property of RG steps that led to
\equatref{eq:RGeigenvalue}, and has nothing to do with the system itself being
scale invariant. Hence we can choose
\begin{equation}
  b=|t|^{-1/y_t}~~~~\text{or}~~~~b=h^{-1/y_h}.
\end{equation} 
If we restrict our attention to the relevant directions by setting $x_i=0$ for all $i\geq3$
we obtain finally the scaling hypothesis:\index{scaling hypothesis!Widom}
\begin{theorem}{Widom scaling hypothesis}{}
  \begin{equation*}\begin{aligned}
    \fsing(t,h)&=|t|^{d/y_t}\fsing\left(\pm1,|t|^{-y_h/y_t}h\right)\\
    \fsing(t,h)&=h^{d/y_h}\fsing\left(h^{-y_t/y_h}t,1\right)
  \end{aligned}\end{equation*}
\end{theorem}

One can see from the scaling hypothesis that the order parameter, its
susceptibility, and $C_V$ will scale according to critical exponents
near the critical point. We choose one or the other of the above forms
strategically depending on the observable we're looking at:
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
  C_V&=\partial_T^2 F
     \sim\partial_t^2 |t|^{d/y_t}\fsing\left(\pm1,|t|^{-y_h/y_t}h\right)
     \sim|t|^{d/y_t-2}
     \equiv|t|^{-\alpha}\\
  m\big|_{h=0}&=\partial_h F\big|_{h=0}
     \sim\partial_h |t|^{d/y_t}\fsing\left(\pm1,|t|^{-y_h/y_t}h\right)\big|_{h=0}
     \sim|t|^{d/y_t-y_h/y_t}
     \equiv|t|^{\beta}\\
  \chi\big|_{h=0}&=\partial^2_h F\big|_{h=0}
     \sim\partial^2_h |t|^{d/y_t}\fsing\left(\pm1,|t|^{-y_h/y_t}h\right)\big|_{h=0}
     \sim|t|^{d/y_t-2y_h/y_t}
     \equiv|t|^{-\gamma}\\
  m\big|_{t=0}&=\partial_h F\big|_{t=0}
     \sim\partial_h h^{d/y_h}\fsing\left(h^{-y_t/y_h}t,1\right)\big|_{t=0}
     \sim h^{d/y_h-1}
     \equiv h^{1/\delta}.
\end{aligned}\end{equation}
One can eliminate the unknown variables $y_h$, $y_t$, and $d$ to obtain
the first two hyperscaling relations.

Nothing in the above derivation assumed the transition was second order; we only
needed a fixed point and its eigenvalues. A second-order transition corresponds
to $y_t,y_h<d$, so that observables diverge as power laws. First-order
transitions\index{phase transition!first-order} are instead controlled by
{\it discontinuity fixed points}\index{fixed point!discontinuity}, where a relevant
eigenvalue takes the special value $y=d$~\cite{nienhuis_first-order_1975}.
For example, the Ising model in $d\geq2$ below $\Tc$ is controlled by the
zero-temperature fixed point. There temperature is irrelevant, and the only relevant direction is
$h$, with $y_h=d$. To see why this produces a jump, use the scaling relation
with only $h$ relevant and choose $b=|h|^{-1/y_h}$:
\begin{equation}
  \fsing(h)=b^{-d}\fsing\left(b^{\,y_h}h\right)=|h|^{d/y_h}\fsing(\pm1).
\end{equation}
By the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ symmetry, $\fsing(h)=\fsing(-h)$, so
$\fsing(1)=\fsing(-1)\equiv-c$, and
\begin{equation}
  \fsing(h)=-c\,|h|^{d/y_h},~~~~~~m=-\partial_h\fsing
  =c\,\frac{d}{y_h}\,|h|^{d/y_h-1}\,\text{sgn}(h).
\end{equation}
Since the spins are bounded, $|m|\leq1$, the exponent $d/y_h-1$ cannot be
negative, so $y_h\leq d$ always. If $y_h<d$, then $m\to0$ continuously as
$h\to0$. If $y_h=d$, then $\fsing=-c|h|$ has a kink, and $m=c\,\text{sgn}(h)$
jumps by $2c$ as $h$ crosses zero; equivalently, $1/\delta=d/y_h-1=0$. That
is, a first-order transition saturates the bound $y_h=d$. We already saw an
example in \secref{sec:fixedPoints}: at the $T=0$ fixed point of the \dimens{1} Ising
chain, $h'=2h$, so $y_h=1=d$, and indeed at $T=0$ the magnetization jumps from
$-1$ to $1$ as $h$ crosses zero.

The converse argument shows why $y_h=d$ is forced. Suppose $f$ has a kink with
spontaneous magnetization $m_0$, so $\fsing\approx-m_0|h|$. Plugging this into
$\fsing(b^{\,y_h}h)=b^d\fsing(h)$ gives $b^{\,y_h}m_0=b^dm_0$, so $y_h=d$
whenever $m_0\neq0$. Physically, deep in the ordered phase all $b^d$ spins
of a block are aligned with the block spin, so the field felt by the block spin is
$h\sum_{i\in\text{block}}\sigma_i=b^dh\,\sigma'$, i.e. $h'=b^dh$.

The same reasoning applies to a temperature-driven first-order transition,
with $y_t=d$. Then $\fsing\sim|t|$, so the energy $\partial_t f$ jumps, which is
the latent heat. Correspondingly $\alpha=2-d/y_t=1$, reflecting a
$\delta$-function in $C_V$, and $\nu=1/y_t=1/d$, which means a finite system has
its transition shifted and rounded over a range $\sim L^{-d}=1/V$.

\subsection{Finite size scaling}\index{finite size scaling}

In \secref{sec:leeyang} we saw that phase transitions happen in the $V\to\infty$
limit. However when looking at real-world, condensed-matter systems, we deal
exclusively with finite volumes. Similarly all lattice calculations are on
configurations stored in computer memory, and are hence themselves finite as
well. On the other hand, we would like our understanding of statistical physics
to say something about the real, finite-volume world, and conversely, we would
like to learn about phase transitions of large systems from finite-volume
lattice calculations. How do we reconcile these seemingly conflicting goals?
This is the domain of {\it finite-size scaling}; in particular we are going to analyze
how our observables change as a function of the lattice extension, which we will
call $L$. For simplicity we consider lattices with geometry $L^d$.

We consider systems with $h=0$. 
Note that, near a second-order critical point, the correlation
length $\xi$ is, according to \tabref{tab:scaling}, expected to scale like
$|t|^{-\nu}$. On the other hand, in a finite box, the correlation length is bounded
from above by $L$. This suggests
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fssnu}
    |\Tc(L)-\Tc(\infty)|\sim L^{-1/\nu}.
\end{equation}
This hard cutoff can also be utilized to guess how some other observables
change as $L$ approaches the thermodynamic limit. For instance, the same
table tells us $\chi\sim|t|^{-\gamma}$. Taking this as inspiration, we
make the ansatz
\begin{equation}
    \chi(L)=|t|^{-\gamma}g\left(\frac{L}{\xi}\right)
\end{equation}
for some function $g$.\footnote{This $g$ is just another scaling function; it
is unrelated to the normalization $g$ of \equatref{eq:widom1}.} To keep our scaling laws consistent, we demand that 
$g$ has the limiting behavior
\begin{equation}
    \lim_{x\to\infty}g(x)=\text{const.},~~~~~~\lim_{x\to0}g(x)\sim x^{\gamma/\nu}.
\end{equation}
The former limit ensures that we recover the correct thermodynamic limit scaling law
for $\chi$ while the latter limit enforces a temperature-independent susceptibility
as the correlation length diverges. This latter limit also shows us that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fss_susc_2nd}
    \chi_{\text{max}}\sim L^{\gamma/\nu}.
\end{equation}
This is extremely helpful, in that it directly permits us to extract a ratio of
critical exponents, even at finite volume where there is no true transition.
Similar arguments yield for the specific heat
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fss_CV}
    C_{V,\,\text{max}}\sim L^{\alpha/\nu}
\end{equation}
and for the magnetization
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fss_M}
    m\big|_{T=\Tc(L)}\sim L^{-\beta/\nu}.
\end{equation}

The above ansatz and its limiting behavior can be justified using the RG
machinery developed above. Rather than sending $L\to\infty$, we
treat $L^{-1}$ as one more coupling. An RG step shrinks all lengths by $b$,
measured in units of the lattice spacing, so $L\to L/b$, or
\begin{equation}
  L^{-1}\to b\,L^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Hence $L^{-1}$ is a relevant scaling field with eigenvalue exponent exactly $1$.
Including it in the scaling relation for $\fsing$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \fsing(t,h,L^{-1})=b^{-d}\fsing\left(b^{\,y_t}t,b^{\,y_h}h,b/L\right),
\end{equation}
and choosing $b=L$ we find
\begin{equation}
  \fsing(t,h,L^{-1})=L^{-d}\,\Phi\left(tL^{y_t},hL^{y_h}\right)
\end{equation}
for some scaling function\index{scaling function} $\Phi$. Taking derivatives
as before and using the relations of \tabref{tab:scaling} with $\nu=1/y_t$,
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
  \chi\big|_{h=0}&\sim L^{2y_h-d}\,\Phi_{hh}\left(tL^{1/\nu},0\right)
                  =L^{\gamma/\nu}\,\Phi_{hh}\left(tL^{1/\nu},0\right),\\
  C_V\big|_{h=0}&\sim L^{2y_t-d}\,\Phi_{tt}\left(tL^{1/\nu},0\right)
                  =L^{\alpha/\nu}\,\Phi_{tt}\left(tL^{1/\nu},0\right),\\
  m\big|_{h=0}&\sim L^{y_h-d}\,\Phi_{h}\left(tL^{1/\nu},0\right)
                  =L^{-\beta/\nu}\,\Phi_{h}\left(tL^{1/\nu},0\right),
\end{aligned}\end{equation}
where subscripts on $\Phi$ denote partial derivatives. At fixed $L$, the
peak of each observable occurs at some fixed value of the argument $tL^{1/\nu}$,
which gives \equatref{eq:fssnu}. The prefactors then give
\equatref{eq:fss_susc_2nd}, \equatref{eq:fss_CV}, and \equatref{eq:fss_M}.
Finally, since $\xi\sim|t|^{-\nu}$, we can rewrite
$L^{\gamma/\nu}=|t|^{-\gamma}(L/\xi)^{\gamma/\nu}$, which recovers the
ansatz with $g(x)=x^{\gamma/\nu}\,\Phi_{hh}(\pm x^{1/\nu},0)$.
This derivation assumes $L$ is large compared to the lattice spacing, and it
neglects the regular part of the free energy. The latter matters for $C_V$ when
$\alpha<0$, where the regular part dominates. The scaling function $\Phi$ is
universal, but it depends on the boundary conditions and the shape of the box.


Equations \eqref{eq:fss_susc_2nd}, \eqref{eq:fss_CV}, and \eqref{eq:fss_M} 
have a practical use of
allowing extraction of critical exponents from real-world simulations,
which must always be carried out at finite $L$.
Once you have two exponents, the rest can be extracted from hyperscaling relations.
They can be also be used to argue that a phase transition is of second order
belonging to some particular universality class. For first-order transitions,
one expects~\cite{fisher_scaling_1982}\index{phase transition!first-order}\index{phase
transition!second-order}\index{crossover}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fss_susc_1st}
    \chi_{\text{max}}\sim L^d.
\end{equation}
This follows from the discontinuity fixed point discussed above: in general
$\gamma/\nu=2y_h-d$, which equals $d$ when $y_h=d$. Similarly $\alpha/\nu=2y_t-d$,
so a temperature-driven first-order transition has $C_{V,\,\text{max}}\sim L^d$.
For a crossover, one expects to see no scaling of susceptibility peak height
at all. Indeed, this strategy was used to demonstrate that the QCD phase change
at physical quark mass and zero baryon chemical potential 
is a crossover~\cite{aoki_order_2006}.


To extract $\Tc(L)$, it is common to use the peak location in $\chi$, the peak
being a remnant of the exact divergence in the thermodynamic limit. Another
possibility is to look for the inflection point in $|\ev{m}|$. These two
estimates are not, to my knowledge, formally guaranteed to agree at any finite
$L$, but they should approach the same $\Tc$ in the thermodynamic limit, as they
both inherit from {\it scaling functions}\index{scaling function} like $g$ that
know where the critical point is. I am not aware of an inherent advantage to
using one over the other, but using $\chi$ seems to be the most common. In
practice, you can estimate your critical parameters using both approaches and
ensure they agree within uncertainty.

\section{Stability conditions}\index{stability}

Here we discuss some phenomenological aspects of system stability following the
nice presentation in Ref.~\cite{debenedetti1996}.

Consider a system in equilibrium described by the control parameters $S$, $V$,
and $N$, i.e. we are in the microcanonical picture. 
By the second law of thermodynamics, this is a
state whose entropy is maximized. It is also a state 
whose internal energy is minimized. These
conditions can be expressed in terms of variations from the equilibrium state:
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
\atFixed{\delta S}{U,V,N} &\leq 0\\
\atFixed{\delta U}{S,V,N} &\geq 0.
\end{aligned}\end{equation}

Now consider an equilibrium system described by $N$, $T$, and $V$. This would
happen e.g. for a system in contact with a thermal reservoir. We consider the
system and reservoir to be divided by a non-adiabatic boundary, which means no
work is done, which is consistent with our control variables. The reservoir and
system then have the same temperature. Consider again variations from the
equilibrium state. The first and second laws respectively tell us
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}\label{eq:stable1}
\delta S + \delta S^R&\leq 0\\
\delta U + \delta U^R& = 0,\\
\end{aligned}\end{equation}
where the $R$ superscript indicates quantities describing the reservoir.
Since the reservoir performs no work and has the same temperature as the system,
its energy change by the first law is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ureservoir}
\delta U^R = T\delta S^R
\end{equation}
Combining \equatref{eq:Ureservoir} with \equatref{eq:stable1} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ureservoir}
\atFixed{\delta F}{T,V,N} \geq 0, 
\end{equation}
i.e. the Helmholtz free energy is minimized for an equilibrium system. A
phenomenological argument using instead a mechanical boundary
shows that the Gibbs free energy should also be minimized in equilibrium.


We want now to study the stability of the system. For a generic free energy $E$
to be minimized, its first derivative w.r.t. a control parameter vanishes, and
its second derivatives must be positive. Let $X$ be a generalized force and
$\xi$ be its conjugate variable. Then 
\begin{equation}
\pdv{E}{X}=\xi,
\end{equation} 
where the partial derivative as usual has the other primary control variables
fixed. Thus the condition that second derivatives are positive is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
\pdv{\xi}{X}>0.
\end{equation} 
At the limit of stability, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stabilityLimit}
\pdv{\xi}{X}=0.
\end{equation}
These imply experimentally accessible stability conditions. For example
substituting $-P$ (due to the sign on the first law) and $V$ we
learn that for stable systems 
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}\label{eq:kTstable}
-V\atFixed{\pdv{P}{V}}{T}=\frac{1}{\kappa_T}>0
\end{aligned}\end{equation}
is a stability condition. Because $P$ and $V$ are thought of as ``mechanical"
parameters, this is sometimes referred to as a {\it mechanical
stability}\index{stability!mechanical}
condition.

\caption{Phenomenological picture of metastability. The image is taken 
from Ref.~\cite{debenedetti1996}, where they denote Helmholtz free energy with
$A$ instead of $F$. See text for explanation.}

\end{figure}

\subsection{Superheated liquids and supercooled gases}

Next we consider the stability of a pure substance near a first-order liquid-gas
phase transition. 
An example could be water, sitting on the curve between the triple
point and $\Tc$. 
The situation is shown schematically in \figref{fig:metastability}. 
Plots (a-c) show some isotherms at temperature $T_1<\Tc$; curves closest
to the symbol $T_1$ in these plots are part of the isotherm. 
The points $b$ and $b'$ label saturated
liquid and vapor phases, respectively. The plots are somewhat complicated
and connected to each other, so we will jump around between them in this
presentation.

The coexistence curve in plot (a)
manifests as a straight line passing through $b$ and $b'$. 
For suppose we have $N$ total degrees of freedom 
of which $N_L$ are liquid and $N_G$ are gas, and let $x$ be the fraction
of liquid. Then $1-x$ is the fraction of gas. The two saturated phases exist
at volumes 
\begin{equation}
V_L=xNv_L,~~~~~~~~V_G=(1-x)Nv_G,
\end{equation}
where $v_L$ and $v_G$ indicate volume per degree of freedom in the respective
phases, and the total volume is $V=V_L+V_G$. Along the coexistence curve, $F$
is a linear combination of $F_L$ and $F_G$, the equilibrium free energies of
the saturated phases, and since $F$ scales linearly
with $N$, 
\begin{equation}
F\propto x F_L + (1-x) F_G.
\end{equation}
Thus varying $x$ traces out a line in this plane. 
Plots (a) and (b) are related by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:FVtoPV}
P=-\atFixed{\pdv{F}{V}}{T,N}~~~\Rightarrow~~~
F(T,N,V_2)-F(T,N,V_1)=\int_{V_1}^{V_2}\dd{V}P.
\end{equation} 
According to this, since $b$ and $b'$ lie on the same line in the $F$-$V$ plane,
they have the same slope, i.e. the same pressure.

Next we turn our attention to plot (b). The ``S-shaped" curve, which is both
dashed and solid, shows the system isotherm ignoring stability. In the region
between $e$ and $f$, the curve has a positive slope, violating
\equatref{eq:kTstable}, i.e. this region is not stable and hence unphysical.
Thus $e$ and $f$ indicate stability limits in all figures.\footnote{These points
manifest as points of inflection in plot (a), which can be seen from
\equatref{eq:stabilityLimit}.}
The only way to connect $b$ and $b'$, remaining on the isotherm and
without violating stability somewhere, is
therefore to proceed along the dashed, horizontal line at $P_1$. 
The two phases coexist
along this line, which is consistent with plot (a) where we saw the slopes at
$b$ and $b'$ were the same, implying the pressure is the same. Thus the
horizontal, dashed line in plot (b) corresponds to the line segment connecting
$b$ and $b'$ in plot (a).

How would one find $P_1$? At the beginning of the section, we argued the Gibbs free
energy $G$ is minimized at equilibrium, i.e. it is minimized when $T$, $P$,
and $N=N_L+N_G$ are fixed. So the first law for $G$ reduces to
\begin{equation}
0=\dd{G}=\mu_L\dd{N_L}+\mu_G\dd{N_G}.
\end{equation}
Since $N$ is fixed, we only can change the particle numbers $N_L$ and $N_G$ by
converting one into the other, implying $\dd{N_L}=-\dd{N_G}$, from which it
follows that $\mu_L=\mu_G$, i.e. coexistence demands a common chemical
potential. In plot (c) of \figref{fig:metastability}, we see curves for the two
phases in the $\mu$-$P$ plane. Where they cross, i.e. where they have a common
$\mu$, defines $P_1$. In this plane, the isothermal path would follow one
of the curves until it hits the kink where $\mu_L=\mu_G$, then proceed
along the other.

Without access to plot (c), we can still use the condition of equal chemical
potentials to determine $P_1$ from plot (b). Taking Legendre transforms of
Gibbs-Duhem\index{Gibbs-Duhem relation} into $F$ and $G$ reveals 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:GibbsDuhemFG}
F=\mu N-PV ~~~~~~\text{and}~~~~~~G=\mu N.
\end{equation}
Thus the integral in \equatref{eq:FVtoPV} along the isotherm $T_1$, when 
inserting the equilibrium conditions $\mu(b)=\mu(b')$ and $P(b)=P(b')\equiv P$,
can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
PV(b')-PV(b)=\int_{V(b)}^{V(b')}\dd{V}P(V).
\end{equation}
This is the statement that the area under the S-shaped curve between $b$ and
$b'$ is zero. Using this equal area procedure is often called the {\it Maxwell
construction}\index{Maxwell!construction}.

Let us return again to plot (a). We have learned that the line passing through
$b$ and $b'$ has constant $\mu$. Using \equatref{eq:GibbsDuhemFG} reveals
that the $y$-intercept of this line is exactly where $F=\mu N=G$, and 
this intercept has been labelled
with $G$ accordingly. The isotherms connecting $b$ with $e$ and $f$ with $b'$
do not have the minimum $F$ in this figure, which is another way of seeing
that the isothermal system prefers to move along the line segment $bb'$.

Now back to plot (b). Starting on the right-most point of the S-shaped isotherm,
and decreasing the volume, we find the pressure increases until it hits $P_1$.
There, the system prefers to condense gas particles into droplets, continuing
at constant pressure until the system is pure liquid. Thereafter, the pressure
increases again, this time more steeply because the liquid is less compressible
than the vapor.

Up to now, we have been considering isotherms, but the plot (b) also shows some
curves where the temperature is allowed to vary. In particular the curve
$bcb'$ is another coexistence curve, this time for various $T$. Indeed as $T$
changes, the curves in plot (c) move, causing the intersection point to move.
The dome-shaped curve $bcb'$ is the locus of these points, sometimes referred to
as a {\it binodal}\index{binodal} curve. At coexistence, as $T$ changes, $b$
and $b'$ move with each other along that curve. As $T$ approaches the critical
temperature, $b$, $b'$, and $c$ will overlap.

One way to see this is as follows. By \equatref{eq:stabilityLimit}, a limit
of stability is where second-order derivatives of the free energy vanish.
One can ask whether the limits of stability are themselves stable. A stable
limit of stability, in the simplest case, will occur analogously to ordinary
stability: For a function to be stable, its first derivative needs to vanish
and its second derivative needs to be positive.\footnote{If its second
derivative vanishes then we need to look out for the first non-vanishing
higher-order derivative.} Here the function is the
second-order derivative of the free energy, so we need the third-order
derivative of the free energy to vanish and the fourth-order to be positive. 
For the conjugate variables $\xi=P$ and $X=V$, using the internal energy $U$,
this translates to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stablestable}
\pdv[2]{P}{V}=0~~~~~~\text{and}~~~~~~\pdv[3]{P}{V}<0.
\end{equation}
Critical points are stable limits of stability, so we see the point $c$
in plot (b) is the critical point, and we learn why the dome-shaped binodal
curve is concave down. At point $e$, only the first equation of
\equatref{eq:stablestable} is satisfied, so we see it is not a stable limit of
stability. The locus of limits of stability for varying $T$, i.e. the curve
$ecf$, is called the {\it spinodal}\index{spinodal} curve. Point $c$ is the only
stable point on a pure fluid's spinodal. 

Along the curve between $b$ and $e$ on plot (b), the liquid has a pressure 
lower than its vapor pressure, which is an example of a {\it
superheated liquid}\index{superheated liquid}. Let us take a moment to understand this
nomenclature. We start by writing Gibbs-Duhem in the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Gibbs-DuhemDifferential}
\dd{\mu}=-s\dd{T}+v\dd{P},
\end{equation}
where $s\equiv S/N$ and $v\equiv V/N$.
Along the coexistence curve, $\dd{\mu(b)}=\dd{\mu(b')}$, so applying Gibbs-Duhem
in this form reveals
\begin{equation}
\dv{P}{T}=\frac{\Delta s}{\Delta v},
\end{equation}
where $\Delta s\equiv s(b')-s(b)$ and $\Delta v\equiv v(b')-v(b)$. The
choice of where to put $b$ and $b'$ is motivated by wanting both $\Delta s$ and
$\Delta v$ to be positive. Vapor is less dense than liquid and has a higher
entropy. Intuitively, the entropy of the vapor should be higher since the
particles are not as constrained by interparticle forces. Alternatively, the
latent heat $L$ must be positive, since you need to dump positive energy to break
bonds, and $L=T\Delta s$. This is the {\it Clausius-Clapeyron
relation}\index{Clausius-Clapeyron relation}, and it implies
\begin{equation}
\dv{P}{T}>0
\end{equation}
along the coexistence curve, i.e. the pressure is a monotonically increasing
function of temperature. So saying the pressure is lower than $P_{\rm
coexist}(T)$ at fixed $T$ is equivalent to saying the temperature is higher
than $T_{\rm boil}(P)$ at fixed $P$. In other words, if I increase the
temperature beyond the boiling point between $b$ and $e$, it remains a liquid,
hence the name. The monotonicity is reflected in plot (d), where all curves are
monotonically increasing with temperature. Similarly, the points between 
$f$ and $b'$ correspond to a {\it supercooled gas}\index{supercooled gas}.

We return to plot (c). The stability of isotherms in this plot can be
seen through as follows: Gibbs-Duhem in the form
\eqref{eq:Gibbs-DuhemDifferential} reveals
\begin{equation}
\atFixed{\pdv{\mu}{P}}{T}=v,
\end{equation}
so differentiating once more w.r.t. $P$ shows
\begin{equation}
\atFixed{\pdv[2]{\mu}{P}}{T}=\atFixed{\pdv{v}{P}}{T}=-v\kappa_T.
\end{equation}
Since \equatref{eq:kTstable} demands $\kappa_T>0$ for stable systems, we learn
that stability requires stable isotherms in the $\mu$-$P$ plane to be concave down.
The curve connecting $e$ and $f$ therefore is very mildly concave up.

Finally, we discuss plot (d). We've been looking at temperatures below $\Tc$, so
the point $c$ is the right-most point for the thick curves. The curve connecting
$b$ or $b'$ and $c$ is the binodal in this plane. The curves connecting $e$ with
$c$ or $f$ with $c$ are spinodals. As discussed earlier, at fixed temperature,
the superheated liquid has a lower pressure than the coexistence (binodal)
pressure, so it is positioned below the binodal. Correspondingly, the
supercooled gas has a higher pressure.

\section{The Binder cumulant}\label{sec:binder}\index{Binder cumulant} 

Equation \eqref{eq:fssnu} gives a way to estimate $\Tc\equiv\Tc(\infty)$.
Another popular method uses the
{\it Binder cumulant}~\cite{binder_finite_1981,binder_critical_1981}, which is defined as
\begin{equation}
B\equiv1-\frac{\ev{m^4}}{3\ev{m^2}^2}.
\end{equation}
This quantity is closely related to the kurtosis. Binder cumulants computed at
different $L$ are expected to cross at $\Tc$. The accuracy of this approach
improves with larger $L$. Two major advantages to using $B$ over
\equatref{eq:fssnu} for the determination of $\Tc$ are that it requires no
fitting and that contamination from regular contributions is greatly reduced.
It is expected that
\begin{equation}
B=\begin{cases} 
    0, & T\gg\Tc\\ 
    2/3, & T\ll\Tc 
\end{cases}.
\end{equation}

\section{Classification of observables}


Owing to the powerful connection with statistical physics, RG techniques are of
considerable use in LFT. For example one can look at the scaling of various
observables near a continuous phase transition to figure out the universality
class of that transition. In order to do so, one has to figure out which
observables are analogous to, say, the magnetization. In this context one
distinguishes between {\it energy-like}\index{energy-like} and {\it
magnetization-like}\index{magnetization-like} quantities. Energy-like
observables are those that have the same scaling behavior as the energy density
in spin systems, while magnetization-like quantities scale like the
magnetization.

In order to utilize these tools, one needs to correctly classify the observable
of interest. In the context of global symmetry breaking, magnetization-like
quantities are those explicitly break the symmetry, while energy-like quantities
remain unchanged.

We take as an example the QCD chiral phase transition and try to classify some
observables and couplings with respect to it. Let us see how the fermionic
number operator in a Euclidean metric $\bar{\psi}\gamma_4\psi$ changes under
chiral rotations~\eqref{eq:SUNfc} and \eqref{eq:U1A}:
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\psi}'\gamma_4\psi'
    =\bar{\psi}e^{i\alpha\gamma_5T^a}\gamma_4e^{i\alpha\gamma_5T^a}\gamma_4\psi.
\end{equation}
The $T^a$ carry no Dirac indices, so they will commute with the $\gamma_i$,
meanwhile $\gamma_4$ and $\gamma_5$ anti-commute. Hence
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\psi}'\gamma_4\psi'
    =\bar{\psi}\gamma_4e^{-i\alpha\gamma_5T^a}e^{i\alpha\gamma_5T^a}\gamma_4\psi
    =\bar{\psi}\gamma_4\psi,
\end{equation}
which tells us the number operator is energy-like and that the quark chemical
potential is an energy-like coupling.
